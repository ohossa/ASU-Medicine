### Essay / Problem‑Solving Questions (with Answers)

**(256)** A child had otitis media. Two weeks later, he presented with ataxia. Radiologic examination showed brain abscess.  
**A. What is the site of the abscess?**  
*Cerebellar abscess (due to spread from otitis media via venous channels).*  
**B. Explain the mode of infection by the brain abscess in this case.**  
*Infection spreads from the middle ear to the cerebellum via the venous channels (e.g., through the sigmoid sinus or direct extension through the petrous bone).*  
**C. What is the method of healing of this abscess?**  
*Healing occurs by encapsulation and organisation; if untreated, it may rupture into the ventricles.*  
**D. Mention another cause of brain abscess.**  
*Suppurative lung disease (bronchiectasis, lung abscess) via haematogenous spread.*  

**(257)** An atherosclerotic man presents with loss of memory. Radiologic examination shows an area of infarction of the brain.  
**A. Explain the cause of infarction in this case.**  
*Thrombosis of a cerebral artery due to atherosclerosis, leading to ischaemic necrosis.*  
**B. What is the type of infarction in this case? Why?**  
*Liquefactive necrosis; because the brain has high lipid content and is rich in hydrolytic enzymes, which digest the necrotic tissue.*  
**C. Mention the type of healing in this case.**  
*Healing by gliosis (astrocytic proliferation) and formation of a cystic cavity.*  

**(258)** A child suffers from headache and vomiting. Radiologic examination shows multiple saccular aneurysms affecting the blood vessels around the circle of Willis.  
**A. Explain the etiology of the aneurysms in this case.**  
*Congenital weakness of the arterial wall (berry aneurysms).*  
**B. If these aneurysms rupture, what is the site of haemorrhage?**  
*Subarachnoid haemorrhage.*  
**C. Mention other causes of cerebral aneurysms.**  
*Mycotic (septic emboli), traumatic, atherosclerotic.*  

**(259)** A child presented with a cerebellar tumor. Biopsy examination showed diffuse proliferation of glial cells with a fibrillary background.  
**A. What is the diagnosis?**  
*Pilocytic astrocytoma (grade I).*  
**B. Mention the grade of the tumor in this case.**  
*Grade I.*  
**C. Enumerate the other grades of the tumor.**  
*Grade II (diffuse astrocytoma), Grade III (anaplastic astrocytoma), Grade IV (glioblastoma multiforme).*  
**D. Describe the histologic picture of the worst grade.**  
*Glioblastoma multiforme: highly anaplastic cells, mitotic figures, pseudopalisading necrosis, and vascular endothelial proliferation.*  

**(260)** A man presented with a space‑occupying lesion in the temporal lobe. Biopsy examination showed highly anaplastic tumor cells with mitotic figures and areas of necrosis.  
**A. What is the diagnosis?**  
*Glioblastoma multiforme (grade IV).*  
**B. What is the grade of the tumor in this case?**  
*Grade IV.*  
**C. Mention the other grades of the tumor.**  
*Grade I (pilocytic), Grade II (diffuse), Grade III (anaplastic).*  
**D. What is the most common malignant tumor of the brain?**  
*Glioblastoma multiforme.*  

**(261)** A female patient had a cerebral tumor which consists of regular cells with clear cytoplasm and distinct cell membrane.  
**A. What is the most likely diagnosis?**  
*Oligodendroglioma.*  
**B. Describe the radiologic picture of this tumor.**  
*Often calcified, well‑demarcated, sometimes cystic.*  
**C. Classify glial tumors.**  
*Astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, ependymomas, and mixed gliomas.*  

**(262)** A child presented with signs of increased intracranial tension. Radiologic examination showed a tumor arising within the 4th ventricle of the brain. Biopsy examination revealed tumor cells with regular nuclei and formed perivascular rosettes.  
**A. What is the diagnosis?**  
*Ependymoma.*  
**B. What is the most common method of spread of this tumor?**  
*Direct spread within the ventricular system and CSF seeding.*  
**C. Mention the variants of this tumor.**

**D. Enumerate the signs of increased intracranial tension.**

**(263)** A child presents with a cerebellar tumor that consists of malignant small round cells with rosette formation.

# Overview Of Nervous System & Spinal Cord External Features & Scalp

**1. Describe the CNS embryological classification (mentioning cavity of each)**

- Hindbrain (rhombencephalon): 4th ventricle  

**2. Describe arrangement of grey and white matter throughout the CNS**  
- Spinal cord: white matter surrounds H‑shaped grey matter

- Cerebral hemispheres: grey matter on surface forming cortex with deep nuclei  

**3. State functions of the CNS**  
- Maintenance of homeostasis  
- Receiving and integrating sensory information  
- Integrating information  
- Controlling muscles and glands  
- Maintaining mental activity  

**4. Describe site and length of the spinal cord**  
- Occupies upper two‑thirds of vertebral canal

- Length ≈ 45 cm

# Anterolateral System

**1. First‑order neuron in the crude touch pathway ends on ……, while the second‑order neuron ends on ……**  
- Ends on laminae IV and VII (nucleus proprius)  
- Ends on ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL) of the thalamus  

**2. Axons of the anterolateral system decussate through which structure?**  
- Ventral white commissure of the spinal cord  

**3. Pathway for crude touch: second‑order axons run in the brainstem through ……**  
- Medial lemniscus  

**4. Injury of the spinal cord can hardly cause loss of which sensation?**  
- Crude touch  

**5. Spinoreticular tract ends on ……**  
- Neurons of the ponto‑medullary reticular formation  

**6. The spino‑reticulo‑thalamo‑cortical pathway is a route for ……**  
- Slow, dull, aching pain and its affective component  

**7. Describe the spinotectal tract**  
- Second‑order neurons from the spinothalamic tract decussate and terminate in the superior colliculi of the midbrain  
- Responsible for spino‑visual reflexes  

**8. First‑order neuron of pain from the face ends on ……, while the second‑order neuron ends on ……**  
- Spinal nucleus of trigeminal nerve in the pons  
- Ventral posteromedial nucleus (VPM) of the thalamus  

**9. Compare the third‑order neuron in fast and slow pain**  
- **Fast pain:** discriminative component; axons from VPL nucleus pass through posterior limb of internal capsule → corona radiata → postcentral gyrus  
- **Slow pain:** affective component; axons from intralaminar nucleus ascend to anterior part of cingulate cortex (limbic system)  

**10. Compare neospinothalamic and paleospinothalamic tracts**  
- **Neospinothalamic:** fast, discriminative pain; fibres laminated (cervical medial, sacral lateral); ascend as spinal lemniscus; terminate in VPL nucleus  
- **Paleospinothalamic:** affective‑motivational pain; fibres from lamina II cross and ascend as spinoreticular tract; relay in intralaminar nucleus of thalamus  

**11. Fast pain ends on ……, while slow pain ends on …… in the spinal cord**  
- Fast pain: lamina I  
- Slow pain: lamina II (substantia gelatinosa of Rolando)  

**12. Mention the site where first‑order neuron fibres of pain and temperature run in the spinal cord**  
- Dorsolateral (Lissauer’s) tract  

**13. Types of fibres of the first‑order neuron in the pain pathway**  
- A‑delta fibres: fast pain  
- C fibres: slow pain  

**14. Describe the pathway of the third‑order neuron in the sensory pathway**  
- Cell body in ventral posterolateral nucleus of thalamus  
- Axon passes through posterior limb of internal capsule → corona radiata → sensory cortex  

**15. The neuron that decussates in the sensory pathway is the …… order neuron**  
- Second‑order neuron  

**16. First‑order neuron cell body is found in ……**  
- Dorsal root ganglion  

**17. Mention stimuli of exteroceptive and proprioceptive pathways**  
- Exteroceptive: crude touch, pain, temperature  
- Proprioceptive: conscious and unconscious proprioception and fine touch

# Spinal Cord Lesion & Blood Supply

**1. Mention the results of complete spinal cord transection**  
- Above C5: death due to paralysis of respiratory muscles  
- Between C5 and T1: quadriplegia  
- Below T1: paraplegia  

**2. Mention the results of hemisection of the spinal cord at different levels**  
- **At the level of lesion:**  
  - Ipsilateral loss of all sensations (interruption of dorsal horn)  
  - Lower motor neuron lesion due to damage of anterior horn cells  
- **Below the level of lesion (ipsilateral):**  
  - Loss of proprioceptive sensations (dorsal white column involvement)  
  - Upper motor neuron lesion (interruption of corticospinal tract)  
- **Below the level of lesion (contralateral):**  
  - Loss of pain and temperature sensations (lateral spinothalamic tract lesion)  

**3. Mention the results of anterior spinal artery occlusion on the spinal cord**  
- Bilateral upper motor neuron lesion below the level of lesion  
- Bilateral loss of pain and temperature sensations below the lesion  
- Preservation of proprioception and fine touch  

**4. Name the arterial circle around the spinal cord**  
- Arterial vasocorona  

**5. The artery of Adamkiewicz arises from which artery?**  
- Eleventh intercostal artery  

**6. Which spinal cord segment is most likely to become ischemic and which has the richest blood supply?**  
- Mid‑thoracic segment: most likely to become ischemic  
- Lumbar segment: richest blood supply  

**7. Enumerate spinal veins**  
- Anterior and posterior median veins  
- Two anterolateral veins  
- Two posterolateral veins running along ventral and dorsal roots  

**8. Spinal veins drain into ……, which communicates with ……. Near the skull, the longitudinal veins communicate with …… and …….**  
- Internal vertebral venous plexus  
- Intervertebral veins  
- Cerebellar veins  
- Cranial venous sinuses

# Cerebellum

**1. Describe the cerebellar notches**  
- Anterior notch lodges the pons and medulla  
- Posterior notch lodges the falx cerebelli  

**2. Describe the primary fissure (fissura prima)**  
- A fissure on the superior surface of the cerebellum  
- Separates the cerebellum into anterior and posterior lobes  

**3. Describe the posterolateral fissure**  
- A fissure on the inferior surface of the cerebellum  
- Separates the posterior lobe from the flocculonodular lobe  

**4. The inferior vermis is found in which structure?**  
- Vallecula  

**5. What are the folds of the cerebellar cortex called?**  
- Folia  

**6. Name the cerebellar nuclei from lateral to medial**  
- Dentate  
- Emboliform  
- Globose  
- Fastigial  

**7. Write a short note on the flocculonodular lobe**  
- Vestibular connections  
- Concerned with equilibrium  
- Formed of two flocculi and the nodule  

**8. Write a short note on the paleocerebellum**  
- Connections are spinal  
- Receives proprioceptive input from the body and a copy of the motor plan from the cortex  
- Functions in coordination of movements  
- Vermis controls trunk muscles bilaterally  
- Paravermal part controls ipsilateral distal limb muscles  

**9. Write a short note on the neocerebellum**  
- Connections are corticopontocerebellar  
- Projects to the contralateral cerebral cortex via the dentate nucleus  
- Concerned with planning and programming of voluntary movements  
- Formed of the lateral parts of the cerebellar hemispheres  

**10. Mention the cerebellar arteries and their origin**  
- Superior cerebellar artery — basilar artery  
- Anteroinferior cerebellar artery — basilar artery  
- Posteroinferior cerebellar artery — vertebral artery  

**11. Describe the venous drainage of the cerebellum**  
- Superior cerebellar veins drain into straight sinus, transverse sinus, and great cerebral vein  
- Inferior cerebellar veins drain into venous sinuses  

**12. Mention the afferent fibres of the inferior cerebellar peduncle**  
- Posterior spinocerebellar  
- Reticulocerebellar  
- Dorsal external arcuate  
- Ventral external arcuate  
- Vestibulocerebellar  
- Olivocerebellar  
- Para‑olivocerebellar  

**13. Mention the efferent fibres of the inferior cerebellar peduncle**  
- Cerebellovestibular  
- Cerebelloreticular  
- Cerebello‑olivary  

**14. Which is the largest cerebellar peduncle and what does it contain?**  
- Middle cerebellar peduncle  
- Corticopontocerebellar fibres  

**15. Mention the afferent fibres of the superior cerebellar peduncle**  
- Anterior spinocerebellar  
- Tectocerebellar  

**16. Mention the efferent fibres of the superior cerebellar peduncle**  
- Fastigioreticular  
- Cerebellorubral  
- Dentatothalamic  
- Dentatorubrothalamic  

**17. Climbing fibres originate from where and terminate on what?**  
- Inferior olivary nucleus  
- Smooth dendritic branches of Purkinje cells  

**18. Fibres from deep cerebellar nuclei project mainly to which structure?**  
- Vestibular nuclei  

**19. Which is the largest cerebellar nucleus and which is the most medial?**  
- Dentate nucleus  
- Fastigial nucleus  

**20. Which cerebellar nucleus is wedge‑shaped and which is rounded?**  
- Emboliform nucleus  
- Globose nucleus  

**21. Describe the corticobulbar projections of the cerebellum**  
- Purkinje cells project mainly to the fastigial nucleus  
- Fastigial nucleus projects to vestibular nuclei and reticular formation  
- Other Purkinje fibres project to dentate, emboliform, and globose nuclei  
- These nuclei project to the red nucleus and ventrolateral nucleus of the thalamus  

**22. The dentate nucleus receives afferents from which nuclei?**  
- Pontine nuclei  
- Inferior olivary nuclei

  - Two roots: motor (large, medial) and nervus intermedius (small, lateral)

**7. Mention the structures winding around the cerebral peduncle**

**11. Describe the boundaries of the interpeduncular fossa**

- Tuber cinereum  

**14. The posterior perforated substance is pierced by which vessels, and the anterior perforated substance by which?**  
- Posterior perforated substance: posterior cerebral arteries  
- Anterior perforated substance: anterior and middle cerebral arteries  

**15. Describe the bound (open) medulla**  
- Shape: triangular  
- Base: medullary striae

**16. Mention the trigones separated by the inferior fovea and their position**

**19. The lower part of the inferior fovea forms which structure?**  
- Area postrema  

**20. Describe the connections of the colliculi of the midbrain**

**21. Describe the roof of the fourth ventricle**  
- Superior part: superior cerebellar peduncles and superior medullary velum  
- Inferior part: inferior medullary velum  
- Median opening: foramen of Magendie connecting the ventricle to the subarachnoid space  

**22. Describe the lateral boundaries of the fourth ventricle**  
- Superiorly: superior cerebellar peduncle  
- Inferiorly: inferior cerebellar peduncle  
- Lateral recess: extends laterally over the inferior cerebellar peduncle  
- Opens into subarachnoid space via foramen of Luschka  

**23. Describe the reticular formation**  
- Network of neurons and fibres scattered within the brainstem  
- Extends superiorly to the hypothalamus and thalamus  
- Extends inferiorly to the spinal cord  
- Neurons arranged in three columns: median, medial, and lateral

# Blood Supply Of Brain Stem

**1. Mention the arteries supplying the medulla oblongata**  
- Anterior spinal artery  
- Posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA)  
- Medullary branches of vertebral artery (supply intermediate area and olive)  
- Posterior spinal artery (supplies posterior part of closed medulla, gracile and cuneate nuclei)  

**2. Describe medial medullary syndrome**  
- Cause: Occlusion of anterior spinal artery  
- Pyramid: Contralateral hemiplegia (UMN lesion, initially flaccid)  
- Medial lemniscus: Contralateral loss of fine touch and proprioception  
- Hypoglossal nucleus/nerve (XII): Ipsilateral LMN paralysis of tongue muscles

- Spinocerebellar tract and inferior cerebellar peduncle: Cerebellar ataxia  
- Spinal nucleus and tract of trigeminal nerve: Ipsilateral loss of pain and temperature from face  
- Spinothalamic tract: Contralateral loss of pain and temperature from body  
- Nucleus ambiguus: Ipsilateral paralysis of larynx, pharynx, and palate  
- Nucleus solitarius: Loss of taste  
- Descending sympathetic fibres: Ipsilateral Horner’s syndrome  

**4. Mention the blood supply of the pons and the effect of its lesion**  
- Blood supply: Branches of the basilar artery  
- Lesion (pontine haemorrhage):  
  - Pinpoint pupils  
  - Hyperpyrexia  
  - Quadriplegia  

**5. Mention the blood supply of the midbrain**  
- Posterior cerebral artery  
- Superior cerebellar artery

- Posterior cranial fossa.

- Sphenomandibular ligament  

**6. Muscles of mastication develop from which pharyngeal arch?**  
- First pharyngeal arch  

**7. Describe the origin, insertion, and nerve supply of temporalis muscle**  
- Origin: Floor of temporal fossa and temporal fascia

- Nerve supply: Two deep temporal nerves from anterior division of mandibular nerve  

**8. Describe the origin, insertion, and nerve supply of masseter muscle**

- Nerve supply: Masseteric nerve from anterior division of mandibular nerve  

**9. Describe the origin, insertion, and nerve supply of lateral pterygoid muscle**

  - Lower head: Lateral surface of lateral pterygoid plate

**10. Describe the origin, insertion, and nerve supply of medial pterygoid muscle**  
- Origin:  
  - Superficial head: Tuberosity of maxilla  
  - Deep head: Medial surface of lateral pterygoid plate  
- Insertion: Medial surface of angle of mandible  
- Nerve supply: Branch from mandibular nerve trunk  

**11. Briefly describe the actions of temporalis and masseter muscles**  
- Both elevate the mandible (antigravity muscles)  
- Temporalis retracts the mandible  
- Masseter assists in protrusion of mandible  

**12. Briefly describe the actions of lateral and medial pterygoid muscles**  
- Lateral pterygoid: Depresses mandible (opens mouth) and protrudes mandible  
- Medial pterygoid: Elevates mandible  
- Together: Produce side‑to‑side chewing movements  

**13. Mention the structures passing between the two heads of lateral pterygoid**

**15. Medial surface of lateral pterygoid muscle is related to what structures?**

- Nerves: Mandibular nerve trunk, otic ganglion, chorda tympani  
- Ligament: Sphenomandibular ligament  

**16. What type of joint is the temporomandibular joint (TMJ)?**  
- Synovial (modified hinge / condylar) joint  

**17. Describe the articular disc of the TMJ**  
- Upper surface: Concavoconvex to fit mandibular fossa and articular eminence  
- Lower surface: Concave to fit head of mandible  

**18. Describe the temporomandibular ligament**  
- Lies lateral to joint capsule

- Protects external auditory meatus  

**19. Describe the stylomandibular ligament**  
- Lies posterior to joint capsule  
- Separates parotid gland from submandibular gland  

**20. Describe the sphenomandibular ligament**  
- Attached to lingula of mandibular foramen  
- Lies medial to joint capsule  

**21. Depression of the mandible is aided by which muscles?**  
- Geniohyoid  
- Digastric  
- Mylohyoid  

**22. Describe the blood and nerve supply of TMJ**  
- Blood supply: Superficial temporal and maxillary arteries  
- Nerve supply: Auriculotemporal and masseteric nerves  

**23. Why does TMJ dislocation occur most commonly in the anterior direction?**  
- TMJ lies anterior to external auditory meatus  
- Lateral temporomandibular ligament prevents backward displacement, protecting the meatus  

**24. Mention the relations of the mandibular nerve trunk in the infratemporal fossa**  
- Medial: Tensor palati and otic ganglion  
- Lateral: Lateral pterygoid muscle  
- Posterior: Middle meningeal artery  

**25. Mention the branches of the main mandibular nerve trunk**  
- Nervus spinosus

**26. Mention the branches of the posterior division of mandibular nerve**

**27. The auriculotemporal nerve passes between which structures?**

- Neck of mandible  

**28. List the branches of the auriculotemporal nerve**  
- Auricular  
- Temporal  
- Glandular  
- Articular  

**29. Describe the course of the lingual nerve**  
- Joined by chorda tympani  
- Passes between mandible and medial pterygoid muscle  
- Crosses styloglossus, hyoglossus, and genioglossus muscles  
- Suspends the submandibular ganglion  

**30. Describe the distribution of the lingual nerve**  
- Ganglionic branches to submandibular ganglion  
- Taste from anterior two‑thirds of tongue and parasympathetic supply to submandibular and sublingual glands via chorda tympani  
- General sensation to anterior two‑thirds of tongue, floor of mouth, and lingual gingiva  

**31. Inferior alveolar nerve ends as which nerve?**  
- Mental nerve  

**32. List the branches of the inferior alveolar nerve**  
- Mylohyoid nerve  
- Mental nerve  
- Incisive nerve  

**33. The maxillary nerve passes through which foramen?**  
- Foramen rotundum  

**34. Describe the extracranial course of the maxillary nerve**  
- Enters pterygopalatine fossa  
- Passes through pterygomaxillary fissure to infratemporal fossa  
- Enters inferior orbital fissure  
- Continues as infraorbital nerve through infraorbital groove, canal, and foramen to face  

**35. List the branches of the maxillary nerve (excluding infraorbital nerve)**  
- In cranial cavity: Meningeal branch to middle cranial fossa dura  
- In pterygopalatine fossa:  
  - Ganglionic branches to pterygopalatine ganglion  
  - Zygomatic nerve (zygomaticofacial and zygomaticotemporal)  
  - Posterior superior alveolar nerve  

**36. Describe the role of the zygomaticofacial nerve**  
- Communicates with lacrimal nerve carrying parasympathetic fibres to lacrimal gland  
- Supplies non‑hairy skin of temple  

**37. Describe the supply of posterior superior alveolar nerve**  
- Mucosa of maxillary air sinus  
- Upper three molar teeth  

**38. List the branches of infraorbital nerve**  
- Middle superior alveolar nerve  
- Anterior superior alveolar nerve  
- Palpebral branches (lower eyelid)  
- Nasal branches (ala of nose)  
- Labial branches (upper lip)  

**39. Middle superior alveolar nerve supplies what, while anterior superior alveolar nerve supplies what?**  
- Middle superior alveolar nerve: Upper two premolar teeth and gums  
- Anterior superior alveolar nerve: Upper two incisors and canine teeth

# Thalamus & Hypothalamus & 3rd Ventricle

**1. Mention the dorsal and ventral parts of the thalamus**  
- **Dorsal part:**  
  - Thalamus  
  - Metathalamus (medial and lateral geniculate bodies)  
  - Epithalamus (pineal body, habenular nuclei, posterior commissure)  
- **Ventral part:**  
  - Hypothalamus  
  - Subthalamus  
- They are divided by the hypothalamic sulcus  

**2. Describe the ends of the thalamus**  
- Anterior end: lies posterior to the interventricular foramen of Monro  
- Posterior end: expanded to form the pulvinar  

**3. Mention the surfaces of the thalamus and their relations**  
- Superior surface: free, related to choroid plexus, forms part of the floor of the lateral ventricle  
- Inferior surface: separated from ventral structures by the hypothalamic sulcus  
- Medial surface: forms lateral wall of the third ventricle; both sides connected by interthalamic adhesion (massa intermedia)  
- Lateral surface: separated from lentiform nucleus by the internal capsule  

**4. Site, afferent, efferent, and function of anterior thalamic nuclei**  
- Site: between the two limbs of Y‑shaped internal medullary lamina  
- Afferent: mammillothalamic fibres  
- Efferent: anterior thalamic radiation to cingulate gyrus  
- Function: part of limbic system (Papez circuit); concerned with recent memory and emotions  

**5. Afferent, efferent, and function of medial thalamic nuclei**  
- Afferent: hypothalamus, amygdala, olfactory cortex  
- Efferent: via medial forebrain bundle to prefrontal cortex  
- Function: part of limbic system; responsible for thinking and mood  

**6. Afferent, efferent, and names of the dorsal tier of lateral nuclei**  
- Afferent: receive input from other thalamic nuclei; multisensory integration  
- Efferent: sensory association areas of the cerebral cortex  
- Nuclei: lateral dorsal, lateral posterior, pulvinar  

**7. Mention afferent and efferent connections of the following nuclei**  
- **Ventral lateral (VL) & ventral intermediate (VI):**  
  - Afferent from cerebellum → efferent to premotor and motor areas (motor planning)  
- **Ventral anterior (VA):**  
  - Afferent from basal ganglia → efferent to supplementary motor area  
- **Ventral posteromedial (VPM):**  
  - Afferent from trigeminal lemniscus → efferent to lower one‑third of sensory cortex  
- **Ventral posterolateral (VPL):**  
  - Afferent from medial and spinal lemnisci → efferent to upper two‑thirds of sensory cortex  

**8. Mention afferent and efferent connections of MGB and LGB**  
- Medial geniculate body: receives auditory input from inferior colliculus; projects auditory radiation to auditory cortex  
- Lateral geniculate body: receives visual input from optic tract; projects visual radiation to visual cortex  

**9. Describe afferent, efferent, and function of:**  
- **Midline nuclei:**  
  - Receive input from midbrain reticular formation; project diffusely to entire cortex; increase cortical activity; part of reticular activating system  
- **Reticular nucleus:**  
  - Receives input from entire cortex; projects to thalamic nuclei; inhibits thalamic activity during sleep  

**10. Describe blood supply of the thalamus**  
- **Arterial:**  
  - Medial and anterior regions – posteromedial group of posterior cerebral artery  
  - Lateral and posterior regions – posterolateral group (thalamogeniculate arteries)  
- **Venous:** Thalamostriate vein  

**11. Describe the site and extent of the hypothalamus**  
- Anterior: lamina terminalis  
- Posterior: vertical plane posterior to mammillary bodies  
- Superior: up to hypothalamic sulcus  
- Inferior: forms base of brain beneath the third ventricle (floor of interpeduncular fossa)  

**12. Mention parts of the hypothalamus**  
- Optic chiasma  
- Tuber cinereum  
- Mammillary bodies  

**13. Enumerate nuclei of the hypothalamus**  
- Suprachiasmatic  
- Supraoptic  
- Infundibular (arcuate)  
- Tuberal  
- Paraventricular  
- Mammillary nuclei  

**14. Mention functions of the hypothalamus**  
- Controls autonomic and endocrine functions  
- Regulates food intake, fluid balance, and body temperature  
- Controls emotions, reproduction, and biological clock  

**15. Describe connection of hypothalamus with prefrontal cortex**  
- Periventricular fibre system connects hypothalamus with dorsomedial nucleus of thalamus (bidirectional)  

**16. Describe connection of hypothalamus with limbic system**  
- Medial forebrain bundle connects brainstem central gray to hypothalamus, amygdala, and septal areas  
- Fornix connects hippocampus to mammillary bodies → thalamus (Papez circuit)  
- Stria terminalis carries afferents from amygdala to preoptic nucleus  

**17. Describe connection of hypothalamus with lower centres**  
- Midbrain tegmentum: mammillary peduncle (afferent) and mammillotegmental tract (efferent)  
- Posterior longitudinal fasciculus carries efferents to autonomic nuclei of brainstem and spinal cord (lateral horn cells)  

**18. Describe connection of hypothalamus with pituitary gland**  
- Hypothalamo‑hypophyseal tracts:  
  - Supraoptic‑hypophyseal (vasopressin)  
  - Paraventricular‑hypophyseal (oxytocin)  
- Infundibular (arcuate) nucleus secretes releasing and inhibiting hormones via hypophyseal portal system to anterior pituitary  

**19. Subthalamus lies between ……**  
- Thalamus and midbrain tegmentum  

**20. Pineal body lies inferior to ……; its base is formed of ……**  
- Inferior to splenium of corpus callosum  
- Base formed by superior lamina (habenular commissure) and inferior lamina (posterior commissure)  

**21. Describe function of habenular nuclei**  
- Receive olfactory input via habenular stria  
- Send output to brainstem visceral nuclei (salivatory, dorsal vagal, ambiguus) via fasciculus retroflexus  
- Responsible for visceral responses to smell  

**22. Mention nuclei of the posterior commissure**  
- Darkschwitsch nucleus  
- Interstitial nucleus of Cajal  

**23. Mention function of the posterior commissure**  
- Connects both superior colliculi  
- Connects both medial longitudinal fasciculi  
- Connects pretectal nuclei of both sides  

**24. Describe boundaries of the third ventricle**  
- Lateral: thalamus and hypothalamus  
- Roof: ependyma with tela choroidea; choroid plexus hangs as two longitudinal elevations  
- Floor: optic chiasma, infundibulum, tuber cinereum, mammillary bodies, posterior perforated substance, midbrain tegmentum  
- Anterior: lamina terminalis, columns of fornix, anterior commissure  
- Posterior: posterior commissure, pineal body, suprapineal recess  

**25. Mention recesses of the third ventricle**  
- Optic recess  
- Infundibular recess  
- Pineal recess  
- Suprapineal recess  

**26. Mention functions of the pineal gland**  
- Endocrine gland secreting melatonin  
- Inhibits pituitary, pancreas, adrenals, gonads, and parathyroids  
- After puberty becomes calcified forming brain sand (radiological landmark)

- Column of fornix

**17. Describe bounds of anterior horn of lateral ventricle**

**20. The choroid fissure lies between …… in body of lateral ventricle, while in inferior horn it lies between ……**

**21. Mention arteries of tela choroidea**

**32. Mention central branches of circulus arteriosus and their supply**

**33. Mention cortical supply of anterior cerebral artery**

**34. Describe supply and lesion of anterior cerebral artery**

**35. Mention cortical areas supplied by middle cerebral artery, functional supply and lesion**

- Supplies lateral orbital surface and temporal pole

**36. Mention cortical areas supplied by posterior cerebral artery, functional supply and lesion**  
- Supplies entire occipital lobe  
- Lower border strip of lateral surface  
- Inferior (tentorial) surface except temporal pole

**37. Describe superficial system of cerebral veins**  
- Superior cerebral veins drain into superior sagittal sinus  
- Superficial middle cerebral vein drains into cavernous sinus and communicates with superior sagittal and transverse sinuses  
- Deep middle cerebral vein drains insula and joins anterior cerebral and striate veins to form basal vein draining into great cerebral vein  
- Inferior cerebral veins drain into nearby sinuses, mainly transverse sinus  

**38. Thalamostriate vein drains into ……, which unites with the opposite side to form ……, which forms …… by uniting with ……**  
- Internal cerebral vein  
- Great cerebral vein  
- Straight sinus  
- Inferior sagittal sinus

# Embryology

**1. Mention sources of mesenchymal core of pharyngeal arches**  
- Paraxial mesoderm which forms musculature  
- Migrating neural crest cells which form skeletal elements or arch cartilage  

**2. Internal grooves of pharyngeal arches are called……, while external are called……**  
- Internal: Pharyngeal pouches  
- External: Pharyngeal clefts  

**3. Mention structures invading each pharyngeal arch**  
- An aortic arch connecting aortic sac with corresponding dorsal aorta  
- A cranial nerve from the adjacent hindbrain  

**4. 1st arch divides into 2 prominences which are……**  
- Ventral mandibular prominence  
- Dorsal maxillary prominence  

**5. Mention derivatives of 1st pharyngeal arch**  
- Skeletal elements form two cartilages: Meckel’s ventral cartilage and dorsal cartilage in maxillary prominence; both disappear leaving incus and malleus near the developing ear  
- Mesenchymal elements around Meckel’s cartilage give mandible by membranous ossification, sphenomandibular ligament, spine of sphenoid, and anterior ligament of malleus  
- Mesenchyme in maxillary process gives maxilla, zygomatic bone, palatine, and squamous temporal bones by membranous ossification  
- Gives all muscles supplied by mandibular branch of trigeminal nerve and the nerve itself  
- Aortic arch forms the maxillary artery  

**6. Enumerate derivatives of the 2nd pharyngeal arch**  
- Reichert’s cartilage gives stapes, styloid process, stylohyoid ligament, lesser cornu, and upper part of body of hyoid  
- Gives all muscles supplied by facial nerve and the nerve itself  
- Stapedial artery  

**7. Mention derivatives of the 3rd pharyngeal arch**  
- Greater cornu and lower part of body of hyoid  
- Stylopharyngeus muscle and glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX)  
- Common carotid artery and internal carotid artery; external carotid artery arises as a sprout  

**8. Mention derivatives of the 4th pharyngeal arch**  
- All laryngeal cartilages except cricoid and epiglottis  
- Cricothyroid, levator palati, and pharyngeal constrictor muscles  
- Superior laryngeal nerve of vagus  
- Arch of aorta on left; arch of subclavian artery on right  

**9. Mention derivatives of the 6th pharyngeal arch**  
- Cricoid cartilage  
- Laryngeal muscles except cricothyroid  
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve of vagus  
- Proximal parts on both sides form pulmonary arteries; distal part disappears on right and remains on left as ductus arteriosus  

**10. General reason for occurrence of 1st arch syndromes**  
- Due to insufficient migration of neural crest cells to the 1st pharyngeal arch  

**11. Describe the following syndromes**  
- **Treacher Collins syndrome**  
  - Autosomal dominant  
  - Underdeveloped zygomatic bone  
  - Defect of lower eyelids  
  - Deformed external ear  
- **Pierre Robin syndrome**  
  - Mandibular hypoplasia  
  - Eye and ear defects  
  - Cleft palate  

**12. Describe fate of 1st pharyngeal cleft**  
- Persists and forms the external auditory meatus and outer surface of tympanic membrane  
- Auricle develops from six hillocks around external auditory meatus which later fuse  

**13. The mesoderm of ….. proliferates and covers …… clefts till it fuses with ……., forming…… that later disappears**  
- Mesoderm of 2nd arch  
- Covers 2nd, 3rd, and 4th clefts  
- Fuses with ectodermal covering of 6th arch  
- Forms cervical sinus  

**14. Explain the following abnormalities**  
- **Preauricular cyst**  
  - Failure of fusion between hillocks of auricle  
- **Branchial cyst**  
  - Failure of obliteration of cervical sinus; may appear along anterior border of sternomastoid  
- **Branchial fistula**  
  - External: failure of fusion of 2nd and 6th arches  
  - Internal: rupture of cervical sinus into 2nd pouch communicating with pharynx  

**15. Mention derivatives of 1st pharyngeal pouch**  
- Tubotympanic recess  
- Distal part forms lining of tympanic cavity, mastoid antrum, and inner surface of tympanic membrane  
- Proximal part forms Eustachian tube  

**16. Mention derivatives of 2nd pharyngeal pouch**  
- Endodermal lining forms epithelial buds penetrating mesoderm forming epithelium of palatine tonsils  
- Invaded by lymphoid tissue in 5th–7th months  
- Remnant forms tonsillar fossa  
- Dorsal part may share in tubotympanic recess  

**17. Mention derivatives of 3rd pharyngeal pouch**  
- Distal part forms inferior parathyroid glands  
- Ventral part forms thymus gland which later descends  
- Thymus invaded by lymphoid tissue in 3rd intrauterine month  

**18. Mention derivatives of 4th pharyngeal pouch**  
- Dorsal part forms superior parathyroid glands  
- Ventral part forms a transient thymic component that disappears  

**19. Mention derivatives of 5th pharyngeal pouch**  
- Forms caudal pharyngeal complex  
- Gives ultimobranchial body  
- Forms parafollicular (C) cells producing calcitonin  

**20. Describe DiGeorge syndrome**  
- Failure of development of 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches  
- Absence of thymus and parathyroids  
- Deformed mouth and nasal clefts  
- Cardiovascular abnormalities  

**21. The olfactory placodes appear during……**  
- 5th week  

**22. Enumerate derivatives of the intermaxillary segment**  
- Labial component: philtrum of upper lip  
- Jaw component: part of upper jaw carrying four incisors  
- Palatal component: primary palate  

**23. There is a solid rod between maxillary prominence and lateral nasal prominence that later gives rise to……, that open into …..and expand cranially forming…..**  
- Nasolacrimal duct  
- Opens into lateral nasal wall  
- Expands cranially forming lacrimal sac  

**24. Lateral parts of upper lip are derived from…… while philtrum from……**  
- Lateral parts: maxillary prominences  
- Philtrum: frontonasal prominence  

**25. Maxillary and mandibular prominences fuse from……**  
- Lateral to medial to decrease size of stomodeal fissure  

**26. Explain and describe the following abnormalities**  
- **Oblique facial cleft**  
  - Failure of fusion between maxillary prominence and lateral nasal prominence  
  - Lacrimal duct persists as open furrow, often with cleft lip  
- **Macro‑ or microstomia**  
  - Over‑ or under‑fusion of maxillary and mandibular prominences  
- **Rare midline anomalies**  
  - Median cleft lip, cleft lower jaw, cleft nose  
  - More common minor forms: cleft chin and micrognathia  

**27. Development of CNS begins in……**  
- 3rd week  

**28. Mention parts of neuroectoderm and their derivatives**  
- Neural plate: brain, spinal cord, motor neurons, preganglionic autonomic fibres  
- Neural crest: parts of PNS, sensory neurons and ganglia, autonomic ganglia, postganglionic nerves  
- Ectodermal placodes: sensory ganglia of cranial nerves, parts of sense organs (lens of eye, inner ear)  

**29. …… triggers formation of neural plate**  
- Notochord  

**30. Raised edges of neural plate form…… then it invaginates forming……**  
- Neural folds  
- Neural groove  

**31. Anterior neuropore closes in…… while posterior in……**  
- Anterior: 25th day  
- Posterior: 27th day  

**32. Cranial parts of neural folds expand forming……, its cavity forms……, while caudal narrow part forms…… and its cavity forms……**  
- Forms three brain vesicles  
- Cavities form brain ventricles  
- Caudal part forms spinal cord  
- Cavity forms central canal  

**33. Neuroepithelial cells differentiate into……**  
- Neuroblasts  
- Glioblasts  
- Ependymal cells  

**34. Mention layers of neural tube and their derivatives**  
- Inner ependymal (germinal) zone: lining of central canal and ventricles

- Outer marginal zone: white matter  

**35. Mention stages of neuroblast development**  
- With central process extending to lumen  
- Apolar  
- Bipolar  
- Multipolar

- Starts in 4th month of intrauterine life

**37. Derivatives of spongioblasts are……**  
- Astrocytes  
- Oligodendroglia  

**38. The sites for crossing of commissural fibres between right and left are……**  
- Ventral lamina  
- Dorsal lamina  

**39. In neural tube, …… contains sensory neurons while …… contains motor neurons, separated by ……**  
- Dorsolateral (alar) lamina: sensory  
- Ventrolateral (basal) lamina: motor  
- Sulcus limitans  

**40. General reason for formation of cervical and lumbar enlargements of spinal cord**  
- Accumulation of nerve cells supplying the limb buds  

**41. Neurons opposite sulcus limitans in spinal cord develop into……**  
- Lateral horn in thoracolumbar region (sympathetic)  
- Lateral horn in sacral region (parasympathetic)  

**42. General reason for formation of posteromedian sulcus and anteromedian fissure**  
- Posteromedian sulcus: occlusion of dorsal part of central canal  
- Anteromedian fissure: bulging of two basal laminae more than floor plate  

**43. During……, the spinal cord fills the vertebral column; at birth it ends at…… while in adults it ends at……**  
- 3rd month: fills vertebral column  
- At birth: opposite L3 vertebra  
- In adults: opposite upper border of L2 vertebra  

**44. Spina bifida is most common in segments……, its most common cystic type is……**  
- L5–S1  
- Meningomyelocele  

**45. Rachischisis of brain is called……, can be detected by……**  
- Anencephaly  
- Increased alpha‑fetoprotein during amniocentesis  

**46. Mention the two earliest flexures in brain development and describe them**  
- Cephalic flexure: between forebrain and midbrain  
- Cervical flexure: between hindbrain and spinal cord  
- Both are ventrally concave  

**47. Pontine flexure forms……, it differs from the previous ones by……**  
- Forms the 4th ventricle  
- Convexity is forward and it does not affect head shape  

**48. Mention developmental changes that occur to rhombencephalon**  
- Cavity dilates forming 4th ventricle  
- Lateral walls rotate like opening a book  
- Basal lamina becomes medial and alar lamina lateral  
- Remnants of sulcus limitans seen in floor of 4th ventricle  

**49. Mention derivatives of basal lamina**  
- Motor nuclei arranged in three columns:  
  1. General visceral efferent (parasympathetic)  
  2. Special visceral efferent (pharyngeal arches)  
  3. General somatic efferent (myotomes)

# CNS PHARMA

## Opioids 1

**1. Mechanism of action of opioids**  
- Act on μ, κ & δ receptors  
- These are Gi protein–coupled receptors  
- Present in CNS & periphery  
- Act at: afferent pain‑conducting fibres → peripheral analgesia; spinal cord → spinal analgesia; brain → supraspinal analgesia; limbic system → euphoria & ↓ emotional response to pain  
- Cellular level: ↓ Ca²⁺ influx presynaptically → ↓ excitatory NT release; ↑ K⁺ influx postsynaptically → hyperpolarisation  

**2. The opioid partial agonist is ….., while the weak opioid analgesic is …..**  
- Partial agonist: Buprenorphine  
- Weak opioid analgesic: Propoxyphene  

**3. ….. & ….. are metabolised into morphine**  
- Heroin  
- Codeine  

**4. Uses of opioids**  
- Analgesic  
- Anaesthetic  
- Acute pulmonary oedema in LVF following MI  
- Antitussive  
- Antidiarrhoeal  

**5. Analgesic uses of morphine**  
- Chronic visceral pain  
- Post‑operative pain  
- Cancer pain  
- Acute trauma  
- Myocardial infarction  

**6. Main effects of morphine**  
- Analgesia  
- Euphoria  
- Sedation  

**7. Inhibitory effects of morphine**  
- VMC → vasodilatation  
- Respiratory centre → ↑ CO₂ → cerebral VD → ↑ intracranial tension  
- Cough centre  
- Uterine tone  
- Thermoregulatory centre  

**8. Stimulatory effects of morphine**  
- Oculomotor nucleus → miosis  
- CTZ  
- GIT & urinary tract: ↑ sphincter tone (spasmogenic), ↓ wall peristalsis  
- Histamine release  

**9. Antitussive & antidiarrhoeal opioids**  
- Antitussive: Codeine, Dextromethorphan  
- Antidiarrhoeal: Loperamide, Diphenoxylate  

**10. Contraindications of morphine**  
- Acute undiagnosed abdominal pain  
- Normal labour pain  
- Closed head injury  
- Biliary colic  
- Enlarged prostate  
- Asthma  
- Extremes of age  
- Hypothyroidism  
- Liver dysfunction  
- Peptic ulcer  

**11. GR: Morphine is contraindicated in normal labour pain**  
- Causes uterine relaxation → delays labour  
- Crosses placenta → fetal respiratory depression → asphyxia neonatorum  

**12. GR: Morphine is contraindicated in closed head injury**  
- Increases intracranial pressure  
- Worsens brain damage  

**13. Uses of methadone**  
- Treatment of opioid addiction  
- Analgesic in severe chronic pain  

**14. GR: Methadone is used in treatment of opioid addicts**  
- Orally active  
- Long acting  
- Replaces morphine/heroin  
- Gradual withdrawal → smoother & less severe withdrawal  

**15. Side effect specific to methadone**  
- Prolonged QT interval  
- Risk of torsades de pointes  

**16. Differences between pethidine & morphine**  
- Pethidine: less constipating, short acting, less respiratory depression in newborn, does not delay labour, atropine‑like actions  
- High doses: accumulation of norpethidine, risk of convulsions, not used >48 hours  

**17. Side effect specific to fentanyl**  
- Chest wall rigidity  
- ↓ Thoracic compliance  
- ↓ Ventilation (high doses)  

**18. Uses of fentanyl**  
- Analgesic in severe pain  
- Anaesthesia: pre‑anaesthetic medication, cardiovascular surgery  
- Conscious sedation  
- Neuroleptanalgesia  
- Neuroleptanaesthesia  

**19. ….. is more potent than fentanyl, while ….. is ultra‑short acting**  
- Sufentanil  
- Remifentanil  

**20. Advantages & disadvantages of tramadol**  
- **Advantages:** less constipation, less respiratory depression, less addiction  
- **Disadvantage:** increased risk of convulsions  

**21. Advantages of buprenorphine**  
- Less addiction  
- Respiratory depression has ceiling effect  
- If respiratory depression occurs → difficult to reverse (high receptor affinity)  

**22. Uses of partial agonists**  
- Analgesic in severe pain  
- Alternative to methadone in opioid addiction treatment  

**23. Drug interactions of opioids**  
- Opioids + CNS depressants → additive CNS depression  
- Pethidine + MAOI → serotonin syndrome  
- Pure agonist + partial agonist → withdrawal + ↓ analgesia  

**24. Treatment of acute morphine toxicity & signs of improvement**  
- **Treatment:** support respiration, naloxone  
- **Signs of improvement:** ↑ respiratory rate, pupillary dilatation  

**25. Uses of naloxone & naltrexone**  
- Acute opioid toxicity  
- Asphyxia neonatorum  
- Opioid abuse  
- Alcohol abuse  

**26. When should naltrexone be given to addicts & why?**  
- After full detoxification  
- To avoid precipitation of withdrawal syndrome  

**27. Steps of treatment of addiction**  
- Symptomatic treatment: NSAIDs for pain, antiemetics  
- Sympatholytic: clonidine  
- Detoxification: methadone or buprenorphine, gradual withdrawal  
- Maintenance: naltrexone (blocks opioid euphoria)

## Parkinsonism

**1. Effect of carbidopa co‑administered with L‑dopa**  
- Decreases L‑dopa dose by 75%  
- Prevents peripheral dopa decarboxylation  
- Decreases peripheral side effects  
- Without carbidopa: 97–99% of L‑dopa metabolised peripherally  

**2. Adverse effects of L‑dopa**  
- **Peripheral:** GIT disturbance, postural hypotension, arrhythmias  
- **Central:** dyskinesia (choreoathetosis), confusion, hallucinations  
- **Fluctuations:** on‑off effect (swings from bradykinesia to mobility), end‑of‑dose akinesia  

**3. Drug of choice in L‑dopa nausea & why**  
- Domperidone  
- Does not cross BBB  
- Blocks D2 in CTZ but not basal ganglia → does not interfere with L‑dopa  

**4. Mechanism of action & adverse effects of ropinirole**  
- Direct D2 agonist  
- Less fluctuation due to rapid absorption & long t½  
- Adverse effects similar to L‑dopa: hallucination, dyskinesia, nausea, postural hypotension, impulse control disorder, daytime sleepiness  

**5. Side effect specific to bromocriptine**  
- Vasospasm  
- Cardiac fibrosis  
- Retroperitoneal fibrosis  

**6. Mechanism of action & adverse effects of amantadine**  
- Increases DA release (mild)  
- NMDA receptor blocker  
- Used in combination due to rapid tolerance  
- Adverse effects: livedo reticularis, ankle oedema, insomnia, hallucinations  

**7. Mechanism of action & adverse effects of selegiline**  
- MAO‑B inhibitor  
- Adverse effects: insomnia, hallucination, very low risk of cheese reaction  

**8. Mechanism of action & adverse effects of entacapone**  
- COMT inhibitor  
- Decreases peripheral L‑dopa metabolism  
- Increases bioavailability & prolongs action  
- Decreases fluctuations  
- Adverse effects related to L‑dopa/carbidopa: hallucination, dyskinesia, nausea, postural hypotension, orange urine  

**9. Indications of apomorphine**  
- Rescue therapy in sudden akinetic episodes  
- Intermittent subcutaneous administration to decrease off‑time in advanced Parkinson’s  

**10. Anticholinergics used in Parkinson’s**  
- Benztropine  
- Trihexyphenidyl  

**11. Uses of anticholinergics**  
- Drug‑induced Parkinsonism (dopaminergic agents cannot be used in antipsychotic‑induced Parkinsonism)  
- Adjuncts to dopaminergic drugs for tremors  
- Control sialorrhoea, especially in young patients  

**12. GR: Anticholinergics should be used cautiously in elderly**  
- Increase memory loss & hallucinations  
- Urine retention in enlarged prostate  

**13. Most effective against motor complications**  
- L‑dopa  

**14. To avoid L‑dopa motor complications, other agents are used first in:**  
- Mild cases  
- When tremor is the only symptom  

**15. Increases peripheral decarboxylation of L‑dopa**  
- Pyridoxine  

**16. Optimum diet for Parkinson’s**  
- Low in protein (amino acids interfere with L‑dopa metabolism)  
- High fibre & fluid intake (to prevent constipation)

## Anxiolytics & Sedative Hypnotics

**1. Drugs with sedative‑hypnotic side effects**  
- Antidepressants  
- Antipsychotics  
- Antihistamines  
- Beta‑blockers  

**2. Classification of GABA receptors with actions**  
- GABA‑A: postsynaptic neuronal inhibition  
- GABA‑B: metabotropic (Gi‑protein linked); presynaptic inhibition by ↓ Ca²⁺ influx; postsynaptic inhibition by ↑ K⁺ outflux → hyperpolarisation  

**3. GABA binding site**  
- Interface between alpha & beta subunits  

**4. BZD binding site**  
- Gamma subunit or area on alpha subunit regulated by gamma  

**5. Alpha subunit actions**  
- Alpha1: sedation/hypnosis  
- Alpha2: anxiolytic & muscle relaxant actions  

**6. Barbiturates vs BZD on Cl⁻ channels**  
- Barbiturates: ↑ duration of Cl⁻ channel opening  
- BZD: ↑ frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening  

**7. Mechanism of action of zolpidem**  
- Binds BZD1 receptor subtype (alpha1) → sedation & hypnosis  
- Facilitates GABA inhibitory effect  

**8. GR: Barbiturates largely replaced by BZD**  
- Narrow therapeutic index → dose‑dependent coma/death  
- No specific antidote  
- More drug dependence  
- Enzyme induction (barbiturates, not BZD)  
- Contraindicated in acute porphyria  

**9. GR: Barbiturates more powerful CNS depressants**  
- GABA‑mimetic action  
- Block AMPA‑R (high conc.) or Na⁺ channels directly  

**10. Ultrashort‑acting barbiturate & dosage form**  
- Thiopental: IV anaesthetic  

**11. BZD classification by duration**  
- Long: diazepam, clonazepam  
- Intermediate: alprazolam, lorazepam  
- Ultrashort: midazolam  

**12. BZD with no active metabolites & IM use**  
- Lorazepam  

**13. BZD metabolism**  
- Oxidation via CYP3A4 → glucuronidation  
- Some directly conjugated by glucuronide  

**14. GR: Clinical duration of long‑acting BZD vs half‑life**  
- Active metabolites prolong effect  
- Receptor dissociation & redistribution to fat/other tissues  

**15. Effect of BZD on sleep patterns**  
- ↓ sleep latency  
- ↑ Stage 2 sleep  
- ↓ REM sleep → ↓ memory consolidation  
- ↓ Stage 3 & 4 NREM  

**16. Drugs metabolised directly by conjugation**  
- Lorazepam  
- Oxazepam  
- Temazepam  

**17. Pharmacological actions of BZD**  
- Anxiolytic/euphoric  
- Sedative/hypnotic  
- Anaesthetic  
- Anticonvulsant  
- Skeletal muscle relaxant  
- Alcohol withdrawal (diazepam, long‑acting)  

**18. BZD uses as anxiolytics & sedatives**  
- Anxiety & panic disorders → potentiate GABA in limbic system  
- Insomnia, nightmares, night terrors → potentiate GABA in brainstem reticular formation  

**19. BZD uses as anaesthetic, anticonvulsant, skeletal muscle relaxant**  
- Conscious sedation, pre‑anaesthesia, balanced anaesthesia → midazolam  
- Epilepsy & status epilepticus → diazepam  
- Muscle spasticity (inflammation, trauma, CP, MS)  

**20. Adverse effects of BZD**  
- Ataxia  
- Anterograde amnesia  
- Abuse  
- Additive CNS depression  
- Paradoxical excitement  
- Drowsiness, confusion, ↓ cognitive function, hangover  
- Withdrawal: rebound insomnia, ↑ REM sleep, nightmares  

**21. Symptoms of BZD toxicity**  
- Prolonged sleep  
- Respiratory & cardiovascular depression (rare except IV or with other CNS depressants)  

**22. Therapeutic uses of flumazenil**  
- Antidote to BZD & zolpidem  
- Termination of anaesthesia effect  

**23. Adverse effects of flumazenil**  
- Agitation  
- Withdrawal syndrome or convulsions in BZD‑dependent patients  

**24. Mechanism of action of zolpidem**  
- Non‑BZD acting on BZD1 receptor subtype → hypnosis  

**25. Advantages of zolpidem over BZD**  
- Rapid onset  
- Less hangover  
- Less cognitive impairment  
- Less tolerance & dependence  
- Less rebound insomnia  
- Less REM sleep suppression  

**26. Similar to zolpidem with fewer cognitive/psychomotor effects**  
- Zaleplon  
- Rapid elimination  

**27. Adverse effects of eszopiclone**  
- Anxiety  
- Dry mouth  
- Headache  
- Peripheral oedema  
- Somnolence  
- Unpleasant taste  

**28. MT agonist: Ramelteon**  
- Hypnotic  
- Adverse effects: dizziness, somnolence, fatigue  

**29. Buspirone: use, advantages, disadvantages**  
- **Use:** selective anxiolytic, chronic/generalised anxiety disorder  
- **Advantages:** no amnesia, no ↓ cognitive function, no additive CNS depression, no ataxia/abuse  
- **Disadvantages:** delayed onset, does not treat insomnia  

**30. Agents for sleep latency vs early morning awakening**  
- ↓ sleep latency: midazolam, lorazepam  
- Early morning awakening: diazepam  

**31. Chronic insomnia treatment**  
- Antidepressants  

**32. GR: BZD added to initial antidepressant therapy**  
- Calms patient until antidepressant becomes effective  

**33. Preferred BZD in liver cirrhosis**  
- Lorazepam  

**34. GR: Dose of BZD in elderly**  
- Reduce to avoid accumulation & prevent falls due to confusion/ataxia

## Local Anesthetics

**1. Compare local anaesthetic esters & amides**  
- **Esters:** broken down by pseudocholinesterases, shorter duration, liable to allergy (PABA derivatives), prolonged in genetic enzyme deficiency  
- **Amides:** longer duration, liable to systemic toxicity, prolonged in liver disease & reduced hepatic blood flow  

**2. Mechanism of action of LA**  
- Bind to receptors on inner surface of Na⁺ channel in nerve membranes  
- Blockade → inhibition of action potential generation & propagation  

**3. Factors affecting nerve sensitivity to LA**  
- Rate of firing (higher in sensory nerves)  
- Nerve size (smaller nerves more sensitive)  

**4. pKa of LA**  
- 7.7–9 (weak bases)  

**5. Factors affecting activity of LA**  
- Plasma protein binding → duration of action  
- Lipid solubility → potency  
- pKa → speed of onset  
- Extracellular pH → infections/repeated injections ↓ activity; NaHCO₃ addition ↑ activity  
- Vasoconstrictors → prolong duration  

**6. Preferred vasoconstrictor in cardiac patients**  
- Felypressin  

**7. Onset & duration comparison**  
- Rapid onset: Lidocaine  
- Longer duration & more potent: Bupivacaine  

**8. Adverse effects of LA**  
- **CNS:** low conc: sleepiness, restlessness, numbness, metallic taste; high conc: tremors, convulsions, respiratory depression, coma, death  
- **Neurotoxicity:** direct local effect of high conc  
- **CVS:** hypotension & bradycardia  
- **Allergy:** esters (PABA derivatives)  
- Side effects of vasoconstrictors added to LA  

**9. Drug interactions of LA**  
- Lidocaine + propranolol → ↓ hepatic blood flow → toxicity  
- Lidocaine + adrenaline + propranolol → severe ↑ BP  
- Lidocaine + adrenaline + TCA → hypertensive crisis  
- Procaine + sulfonamides → ↓ effect of sulfonamides  

**10. Drug for surface anaesthesia**  
- Lidocaine  

**11. Dentistry technique**  
- Nerve block  

**12. Uses of spinal anaesthesia**  
- Surgery of abdomen, pelvis, legs when general anaesthesia contraindicated  

**13. Side effects of spinal anaesthesia**  
- Headache (CSF leakage & traction on sensitive structures)  
- Hypotension (sympathetic block)  
- Spinal root injury  
- Infection  

**14. Uses of epidural anaesthesia**  
- Obstetrics for painless labour

## General Anesthesia

**1. Importance of pre‑anaesthetic medication**  
- Amnesia, ↓ anxiety, facilitate induction  
- Suppress autonomic reflexes, ↓ salivary & bronchial secretion, ↓ gastric motility  
- Potentiate weak anaesthetics  

**2. Inhalation vs IV anaesthetics**  
- Inhalation: maintenance  
- IV: induction  

**3. General anaesthetics acting on glutamate & receptor**  
- Ketamine, Nitrous oxide  
- Receptor: NMDA antagonist  

**4. Factors affecting MAC**  
- Decreased by: opioids, sedative‑hypnotics, clonidine  
- Increased by: alcohol abuse, catecholamines  

**5. Adverse effects of halothane**  
- Hypotension, bradycardia/arrhythmia  
- Hepatotoxicity, malignant hyperthermia  
- Respiratory depression, ↓ mucociliary function  
- Uterine relaxation → bleeding  
- ↑ ICP → CI in head injury & cerebral tumours  

**6. Inhalation anaesthetic considerations**  
- May precipitate ischaemia in coronary patients: Isoflurane  
- Used for day surgery: Desflurane  

**7. New halogenated agent & precautions**  
- Sevoflurane: not a respiratory irritant  
- Precaution: degraded by soda lime → nephrotoxic compound A  

**8. Uses of nitrous oxide**  
- Low potency anaesthetic  
- Effective analgesic (dental, obstetric)  
- Safest inhalation agent  

**9. Adverse effects of N₂O**  
- Expands closed gas spaces → ↑ pressure in middle ear (CI: bowel obstruction, pneumothorax)  
- Hypoxia  
- Abuse, euphoria  
- Megaloblastic anaemia, abortion with chronic exposure  

**10. Clinical uses of thiopental**  
- IV induction for major operations, followed by inhalation maintenance  
- Alone for short procedures  

**11. Adverse effects of thiopental**  
- Respiratory & CV depression (toxic doses)  
- Severe vasospasm if injected intra‑arterially → gangrene  
- Can precipitate porphyria  

**12. Propofol: uses, advantages, disadvantages**  
- **Uses:** induction & maintenance, conscious & deep sedation  
- **Advantages:** antiemetic  
- **Disadvantages:** respiratory & CV depression, pain at injection site  

**13. Uses of ketamine**  
- Dissociative anaesthesia  
- Profound analgesia  
- CV stimulant → useful in shock  
- Potent bronchodilator  

**14. Disadvantages of ketamine**  
- ↑ cerebral blood flow & ICP → avoid in head injury  
- Hallucinations, disorientation, emergence phenomena (blocked by diazepam)  

**15. GR: BZDs more used in sedation than anaesthesia**  
- Prolonged amnesia & sedation at anaesthetic doses  

**16. Uses of BZDs**  
- Conscious sedation  
- Pre‑anaesthesia  
- Balanced anaesthesia, adjuncts  

**17. Technique of total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA)**  
- Combination of opioids + propofol rather than inhalation agent  

**18. Anaesthetic used IM**  
- Ketamine

## Epilepsy And Migraine

**1. Effect of Na⁺ channel blockade & agents**  
- ↓ repetitive firing of neurons  
- Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine, Topiramate, Valproate  

**2. Effect of Ca²⁺ T channel blockade & agents**  
- ↓ brain rhythmic activity  
- Ethosuximide, Valproate  

**3. Effect of presynaptic voltage‑gated Ca²⁺ channel blockade & agents**  
- ↓ glutamate release  
- Lamotrigine, Pregabalin, Gabapentin  

**4. Acts on VS2A vs NMDA receptor**  
- VS2A: Levetiracetam  
- NMDA receptor: Valproate  

**5. Drugs affecting GABA**  
- Facilitate GABA action: Phenobarbital, BZDs, Topiramate  
- Increase GABA level: Valproate (↑ turnover)  

**6. First‑line treatment:**  
- Partial: Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine  
- Tonic‑clonic: Valproate, Lamotrigine  
- Absence: Ethosuximide, Valproate  
- Myoclonic: Valproate  

**7. Phenytoin is used in**  
- Generalised tonic‑clonic and partial seizures  

**8. Advantages of newer agents**  
- Fosphenytoin: IV/IM, less phlebitis & cardiac effects, better bioavailability, used in SE  
- Oxcarbazepine: less potent, less enzyme induction & blood dyscrasias  

**9. Phenobarbital & BZDs use**  
- Status epilepticus & acute fits  
- Sedation & tolerance  

**10. CI in absence & myoclonic seizures**  
- Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Oxcarbazepine  

**11. May worsen myoclonic seizures**  
- Gabapentin, Pregabalin, Lamotrigine  

**12. Hypersensitivity & GIT side effects**  
- Skin rash → stop drug  
- Nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain (esp Valproate) → give small dose after meals  

**13. Neurological side effects**  
- Phenytoin: nystagmus, diplopia, ↓ learning in children, drowsiness  
- Carbamazepine: diplopia, drowsiness  
- Valproate: less sedative, fine hand tremors  
- All: ataxia  

**14. Microsomal enzyme induction side effects**  
- Phenytoin: induces AE, vit D metabolism → osteomalacia (supplement vit D & Ca²⁺)  
- Carbamazepine: induces AE & warfarin metabolism  
- Valproate: induces AE metabolism  

**15. Haematological side effects**  
- Phenytoin: megaloblastic anaemia (folate), lymphadenopathy  
- Carbamazepine: leukopenia, agranulocytosis  
- Valproate: thrombocytopenia  

**16. Teratogenic effects**  
- Phenytoin: cleft palate/lip, cardiac abnormalities  
- Carbamazepine: less teratogenic  
- Valproate: spina bifida, neural tube defect  

**17. Hair effects**  
- Hirsutism: Phenytoin  
- Hair loss: Valproate  

**18. Hormonal imbalance by carbamazepine**  
- Water intoxication & dilutional hyponatraemia → potentiates ADH  

**19. Fatal hepatotoxicity**  
- Valproate  

**20. Causes polycystic ovaries**  
- Valproate  

**21. Side effects:**  
- Topiramate: renal stones, myopia, weight loss, hypohidrosis  
- Lamotrigine: rash → Stevens‑Johnson syndrome  

**22. Drug interactions of lamotrigine**  
- Valproate: inhibits metabolism  
- OCP & carbamazepine: increase metabolism  

**23. Treatment termination & withdrawal**  
- Epilepsy stops: 2 years → terminate  
- Drug withdrawal: over 6 months  

**24. GR: Phenytoin level unpredictable**  
- Irregular bioavailability  
- Saturation kinetics  
- Drug interactions  

**25. Management of status epilepticus**  
- Rapid control: Lorazepam, Midazolam, Diazepam  
- Maintenance: Fosphenytoin or Phenytoin  
- 2nd choice: Phenobarbital  
- IV antiepileptics: Valproate, Levetiracetam  
- Resistant cases: IV anaesthesia  

**26. AE used in:**  
- Mood stabilisers: Lamotrigine, Valproate, Carbamazepine  
- Neuropathic pain: Carbamazepine, Gabapentin, Pregabalin  

**27. Mechanism of action of triptans**  
- Selective 5HT1B/1D agonist  
- Presynaptic trigeminal nerve → inhibit VD neuropeptides  
- VC of dural vessels → prevent pain nerve stretching  

**28. Adverse effects & CI of sumatriptan**  
- Injection site reaction or unpleasant taste  
- Chest pressure (resolves in 30 min)  
- Serotonergic drug interaction: SSRIs (avoid within 24 hrs)  

**29. Mechanism of action & adverse effects of ergots**  
- MOA: similar to triptans  
- AD: GIT upset, chest pressure, vasospasm → gangrene  
- CI: pregnancy, avoid long‑term use  

**30. Migraine prophylaxis drugs**  
- Beta‑blockers: propranolol, timolol  
- Antidepressants: amitriptyline  
- Anticonvulsants: valproate, topiramate  
- CCB: nifedipine, verapamil  

**31. Most common brain tumours (decreasing frequency)**  
- Adults: metastases, glioma, meningioma, schwannoma  
- Children: pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, ependymoma  

**32. GR: Anatomical site influences outcome**  
- Local effects: benign meningioma may cause cardiorespiratory arrest (medullary compression)  
- Non‑resectability: brainstem gliomas  

**33. GR: Complete resection of diffuse glioma usually impossible**  
- Single‑cell infiltration of normal brain beyond tumour core

## Antidepressants

**1. General mechanism of action of antidepressants**  
- ↑ brain biogenic amines  
- Downregulation of beta & 5‑HT2 receptors (delayed 2–4 weeks)  
- Affect neurotrophic factors essential for neuron survival & function  

**2. Amine pump inhibitor antidepressants**  
- TCA: Imipramine, Amitriptyline, Clomipramine  
- SSRI: Fluoxetine  
- NDRI: Bupropion  
- SNRI: Venlafaxine, Duloxetine  

**3. Mechanism of mirtazapine**  
- ↑ NA & 5‑HT release by blocking alpha2 autoinhibition  

**4. Adverse effects of TCA**  
- Autonomic blockade (alpha, H1, muscarinic)  
- ↑ body weight  
- Cardiotoxicity (conduction defects & arrhythmias) & convulsions  
- Sexual dysfunction, switch to mania, serious drug interactions, low safety margin  

**5. Advantages & disadvantages**  
- **SSRI:** Adv: less cardiotoxic, no autonomic side effects, safer in overdose; Dis: serotonin syndrome (esp with MAOI), nausea, anxiety, insomnia, tremors, sexual dysfunction, suicidal tendency  
- **Bupropion:** Adv: no sexual dysfunction, ↑ NE/DA → dec craving, used in smoking cessation; Dis: ↑ risk of convulsions  

**6. Advantages & disadvantages**  
- **Venlafaxine:** Adv: no autonomic side effects; Dis: nausea, sexual dysfunction, dry mouth, hypertension  
- **Mirtazapine:** Adv: less nausea & sexual dysfunction; Dis: H1 sedation & antiemetic effect (5HT3 in CTZ)  

**7. Drug interactions of TCA**  
- Alcohol, anaesthetics → sedation  
- Hypotensive drugs → ↑ postural hypotension  
- Potentiate directly acting sympathomimetics (↓ uptake of biogenic amines)  
- Avoid combination with MAOI → serotonin syndrome  

**8. TCA toxicity & management**  
- **Toxicity:** atropine‑like effects, convulsions, conduction block & arrhythmias, hypotension  
- **Management:** NaHCO₃ for hypotension & arrhythmias  

**9. MAOI interactions**  
- Tyramine & cold remedies → hypertensive crisis  
- TCA → hypertension, convulsions, hyperthermia  
- SSRI → serotonin syndrome, convulsions, hyperthermia  

**10. GR: MAOI in elderly**  
- Avoid due to postural hypotension & atropine‑like effects  

**11. GR: Preferred MAOI – Moclobemide**  
- Selective MAOA → allows MAOB to metabolise tyramine  
- Short‑reversible inhibitor, easily displaced by tyramine  

**12. Agomelatine: advantages & disadvantages**  
- **Adv:** ↑ NE release via 5HT2C antagonism, used in MDD non‑responders or SSRI‑intolerant, corrects sleep disturbances  
- **Dis:** hepatotoxicity  

**13. Therapeutic uses of antidepressants**  
- Major depressive disorder  
- Anxiety disorders (SSRI, clomipramine)  
- PTSD  
- Pain disorders, neuropathic pain (Amitriptyline, TCA & SNRI)  
- Urinary incontinence (Duloxetine), nocturnal enuresis (Imipramine)  
- Eating disorders (SSRI & TCA)  
- Smoking cessation (Bupropion)  

**14. Duration of treatment after remission**  
- Continue: 6 months  
- Withdrawal: over 4 weeks  

**15. Cases for MAOI use**  
- Atypical depression with obsessions, anxiety, phobias, panic attacks  
- 2nd choice in refractory major depression  

**16. Add‑on drugs in resistant cases**  
- Atypical antipsychotics, T3, lithium  

**17. Potent enzyme inhibitors**  
- Fluoxetine & Paroxetine

## Neurodegenerative Disorders

**1. Members of Cholinesterase Inhibitors (ChEI)**  
- Donepezil (long acting)  
- Rivastigmine (short acting, available as transdermal patch)  

**2. Mechanism of action of ChEI**  
- ↑ cholinergic transmission by inhibiting AChE  
- Orally active, cross BBB, selective for central AChE  
- Improve cognitive function but do not delay disease progression  

**3. Uses of ChEI**  
- Mild, moderate, severe Alzheimer’s disease  
- Parkinsonism dementia (Rivastigmine only)  

**4. Side effects of ChEI**  
- Cholinomimetic effects  
- Drug interactions via CYP450 (e.g., Rivastigmine)  

**5. NMDA receptor blockers: mechanism & uses**  
- Non‑competitive NMDA antagonist → limits Ca²⁺ influx → protects neurons from excitotoxicity  
- Used in moderate to severe Alzheimer’s disease  

**6. Side effects of Memantine**  
- Confusion, dizziness, headache, agitation, hallucination  
- Constipation  

**7. Agents for depression & psychosis in AD**  
- Aripiprazole, Olanzapine, Risperidone  

**8. Components of Namzaric**  
- Memantine + Donepezil  

**9. Improves walking in MS**  
- Fampridine  

**10. Management of acute MS exacerbations**  
- Methylprednisolone 0.5–1 g/day IV for 5 days → taper with oral prednisolone  

**11. Adverse effects of corticosteroids**  
- **Catabolic:** carbs: hyperglycaemia (DM); fat: redistribution to face, abdomen, shoulders; protein: muscle wasting, thinning skin, growth retardation, osteoporosis  
- **Salt & water retention:** oedema, ↑ weight, hypertension  
- **Behavioural:** psychosis, depression  
- Peptic ulcer, infection, poor wound healing, hirsutism  

**12. 1st line DMD in MS & mechanism**  
- Interferon‑β  
- Immunomodulatory → reduce inflammatory responses causing demyelination  

**13. Adverse effects of Interferon‑β**  
- Flu‑like symptoms  
- Depression  
- Local injection site reactions  
- ↑ hepatic enzymes (AST & ALT)  

**14. First oral DMD approved & mechanism**  
- Fingolimod  
- Binds S1P1 receptor → alters lymphocyte migration → fewer lymphocytes in CNS, T‑cell sequestration in LN  

**15. Adverse effects of Fingolimod**  
- 1st dose bradycardia  
- ↑ infection risk  
- Macular oedema (caution in DM)  

**16. Mechanism & indication of Natalizumab**  
- Monoclonal Ab against α4β1 → ↓ BBB invasion by T‑cells  
- Indicated for progressive MS after failed 1st line treatment  

**17. Adverse effects of Natalizumab & recommendations**  
- Allergic reaction  
- Anti‑natalizumab Abs  
- Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy  
- Recommendations: test for JC virus; avoid in immunocompromised; do not use >24 months; monitor patient  

**18. First FDA‑approved drug for ALS & mechanism**  
- Riluzole  
- Inhibits glutamate release, Na⁺ channels, NMDA receptor → improves survival & delays ventilation requirement  

**19. Diffuse axonal injury: definition & locations**  
- Injury of white matter with axonal swelling from acceleration/deceleration  
- Locations: corpus callosum, periventricular white matter, hippocampus, cerebral & cerebellar peduncles  

**20. Epidural vs Subdural haematoma: location & vessels**  
- Epidural: between dura & skull, middle meningeal artery rupture, often temporoparietal fracture  
- Subdural: between dura & arachnoid, bridging vein rupture (risk factors: brain atrophy, anticoagulants, shaking, whiplash)  

**21. Epidural vs Subdural haemorrhage: symptoms & CT**  
- Epidural: lucid interval → loss of consciousness (“talk & die”), transtentorial herniation, biconvex haemorrhage, does not cross sutures  
- Subdural: slow venous bleed, gradual headache/confusion, crescent‑shaped haemorrhage, crosses sutures, gyri preserved, cannot cross tentorium

## Antipsychotics

**1. Role of dopamine receptors in schizophrenia & drug effects**  
- Positive symptoms: ↑ dopamine activity on D2 receptor in mesolimbic pathway  
- Negative symptoms: ↓ dopamine activity on D2 receptor in PFC/mesocortical pathway  
- All antipsychotics block D2 receptor  
- Atypical antipsychotics block 5‑HT2A → ↑ dopamine in PFC  

**2. Advantages of typical antipsychotics**  
- Cheaper  
- Fast‑acting IM (Haloperidol) & inhalation for agitation  
- Long‑acting depot injections for non‑adherent patients  

**3. Uses of typical antipsychotics**  
- **Antipsychotic:** violent patients; primary psychosis: schizophrenia, mania, psychotic depression; secondary psychosis: brain tumour, cocaine, dementia  
- Antiemetic  

**4. Side effects of antipsychotics (D2 blockade)**  
- Extrapyramidal side effects  
- ↑ prolactin → gynaecomastia, galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, infertility  
- ↑ body weight  

**5. Autonomic blockade effects**  
- H1: sedation, ↑ body weight  
- M: atropine‑like side effects  
- α: postural hypotension  

**6. Miscellaneous side effects**  
- Cardiotoxicity (↑ QT)  
- Convulsions  
- Corneal/lens deposits  
- Cholestatic jaundice  

**7. Extrapyramidal side effects & mechanism**  
- Akathisia, dystonia → D2 blockade in basal ganglia  
- Parkinsonism → D2 blockade + relative ↑ cholinergic activity  
- Tardive dyskinesia → DA receptor supersensitivity after chronic blockade  
- Neuroleptic malignant syndrome → rapid D2 blockade in susceptible patients  

**8. Management of extrapyramidal side effects**  
- Akathisia: Propranolol  
- Anticholinergics (Benztropine) prophylaxis; may worsen tardive dyskinesia  

**9. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: symptoms & management**  
- **Symptoms:** muscle rigidity, hyperpyrexia, stupor, altered mental status, myoglobinemia, unstable BP  
- **Management:** discontinue antipsychotic, muscle relaxant (dantrolene, diazepam), antiparkinsonian drugs, DA agonist (bromocriptine)  

**10. High vs low potency typical antipsychotics**  
- Low: chlorpromazine, thioridazine → less central side effects, more autonomic & miscellaneous side effects  
- High: haloperidol, fluphenazine → more central side effects, less autonomic/miscellaneous; preferred in elderly/cardiac patients  

**11. Advantages of atypical antipsychotics**  
- Less central side effects: ↓ extrapyramidal & prolactin‑induced effects  
- Improve negative symptoms  

**12. Clozapine**  
- Effective in resistant cases  
- Disadvantages: agranulocytosis, seizures, ↑ autonomic side effects, weight gain, insulin resistance  
- Least central side effects  

**13. Disadvantages of Risperidone**  
- ↑ prolactin & QT interval  

**14. Weight & Parkinson preference**  
- Most weight gain: Olanzapine  
- Parkinson with psychosis & off‑label hypnotic: Quetiapine  

**15. Advantages & disadvantages of Aripiprazole**  
- More extrapyramidal side effects (esp akathisia)  
- Favourable cognitive profile (preferred in children)  
- Less effect on weight & lipids (preferred in obese/DM patients)  

**16. Specific uses of antipsychotics**  
- Tranquiliser: Haloperidol  
- Severe emesis/intractable hiccups: Chlorpromazine  
- Autism: Risperidone & Aripiprazole  
- Refractory depression: Aripiprazole & Quetiapine  
- Off‑label hypnotics: Quetiapine, Clozapine, Chlorpromazine  

**17. Uses of Lithium Carbonate**  
- Mood stabiliser, anti‑manic  
- Recurrent endogenous depression  
- Adjunct to antidepressants in refractory unipolar depression  

**18. First‑line treatment for bipolar depression**  
- Lithium or Lamotrigine  

**19. Adverse effects of Lithium**  
- CNS: fine hand tremors, ↓ cognition  
- GIT upset  
- Renal: polyuria, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, tubular damage  
- Thyroid: benign enlargement, hypothyroidism  
- Toxicity: narrow TI (0.6–1.2 mEq/L; >1.5 mEq/L toxic), long t½, cumulative  

**20. Precautions with Lithium**  
- Early toxicity signs: coarse tremors, ↑ GIT symptoms  
- Monitor kidney & thyroid function  
- Monitor serum lithium level  
- Adjust dose in Na depletion, renal dysfunction, old age  

**21. Manifestations of Lithium toxicity**  
- Early: vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremors  
- Later: ataxia, drowsiness, slurred speech, arrhythmia, CV collapse  
- Severe: confusion, convulsions, coma  

**22. Management of Lithium toxicity**  
- Stop Lithium, gastric lavage, activated charcoal (if acute)  
- ↑ fluid intake, maintain electrolytes  
- Diuresis: osmotic or forced (avoid thiazide/loop diuretics)  
- Dialysis: haemodialysis preferred if severe or fluid intake contraindicated, repeat every 6–8 hrs

## Drug Abuse

**1. Effect of psychoactive drugs on reward system**  
- Heroin, alcohol, cannabis, tobacco → act on VTA → release DA into NAc → euphoria  
- Alcohol → releases endogenous opioids  
- BZDs → stimulate DA in VTA, enhance euphoric effect of other drugs  
- Amphetamine & cocaine → act directly on NAc terminals → ↑ DA release and/or inhibit reuptake  

**2. Reward deficiency & withdrawal**  
- Chronic drug use → dopaminergic neuron degeneration → chronic DA deficiency → functional impairment → craving via +ve & –ve reinforcement  
- Withdrawal involves CRF & NA neurons in amygdala  
- Heroin withdrawal → sympathetic overactivity, ↑ HR & BP  

**3. Management of BZD & barbiturate dependence**  
- Replace short‑acting agents with longer‑acting agents → smoother withdrawal  

**4. Symptoms of alcohol withdrawal**  
- Delirium tremens, delirium, tremors, psychosis  

**5. Management of alcohol abuse**  
- Diazepam or chlordiazepoxide (long‑acting sedatives & anticonvulsants)  
- Thiamine supplements  
- Psychotherapy  
- Detoxification: disulfiram (acetaldehyde accumulation → vomiting, nausea, flushing), naltrexone, acamprosate (↓ craving)  

**6. Management of opioid abuse**  
- Methadone or buprenorphine instead of short‑acting agents  
- Naltrexone  
- Symptomatic: anxiolytics, antiemetics, antispasmodics, clonidine  

**7. Mechanism of nicotine action**  
- Stimulates α4β2 nicotinic ACh receptors in VTA → DA release in reward pathway → reward, craving, dependence  

**8. Management of nicotine abuse**  
- Psychotherapy  
- Nicotine replacement  
- Bupropion  
- Varenicline (partial agonist at α4β2 nicotinic ACh receptors)  

**9. Cocaine: injection vs intranasal**  
- Injection → intense euphoria, ↑ infection risk  
- Intranasal → less euphoria, nasal septum perforation  

**10. Risks associated with LSD abuse**  
- Bad trips  
- Flashbacks  
- Psychosis  

**11. Management of LSD abuse**  
- Calm patient during bad trips  
- BZDs or antipsychotics  

**12. Risks of chronic cannabis abuse**  
- ↑ risk of psychosis  
- Red eyes  
- Apathy  
- Sexual & reproductive dysfunction, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy  
- Gateway drug  
- Immune system impairment  

**13. Management of cannabis abuse**  
- Naltrexone  
- Buspirone or fluoxetine  
- Dronabinol  

**14. Anti‑emetics in cancer chemotherapy**  
- Dronabinol or nabilone

## Energy Sources Of The Brain

**1. Types of brain fuel**  
- Glucose  
- Monocarboxylic acids  
- Ketone bodies  

**2. Major sources of carbon in gluconeogenesis**  
- Lactate  
- Amino acids  
- Glycerol  

**3. When does the brain use lactate as fuel?**  
- During sustained physical exercise  

**4. Glucose in brain is phosphorylated by**  
- Hexokinase  

**5. Glucose uptake**  
- Astrocytes: GLUT1  
- Neurons: GLUT3  

**6. Importance of astrocyte‑neuron lactate shuttle**  
- Bypasses the slower glycolytic rate of neurons  
- (neurons lack 6‑phosphofructo‑2‑kinase/fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphatase‑3 which activates PFK‑1) due to constant proteasomal degradation in neurons  

**7. Lactate shuttling from astrocytes to neurons**  
- Via monocarboxylic transporters  

**8. Glycogen is under dynamic control of**  
- Neurotransmitters  
- Local energy status  

**9. Importance of PPP in brain**  
- Maintains glutathione in reduced form → detoxification of ROS  

**10. Importance of brain energy metabolism**  
- Maintains/restores ion gradients dissipated by signalling processes (postsynaptic potentials, action potentials, neurotransmitter uptake/recycling) are the main processes contributing to the high brain energy needs  
- Brain function is highly dependent on energy from food

## Neurotransmitters

**1. Classify neurotransmitters according to structure & function**  
- Structure (S): amino acids, biogenic amines, neuropeptides, others  
- Function (F): excitatory, inhibitory, or both  

**2. Methods of inactivation of neurotransmitters and therapeutic application**  
- Reuptake: targeted by SSRIs  
- Hydrolysis: targeted by MAO inhibitors  

**3. Principal excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters and their removal**  
- Excitatory (E): glutamate, removed by reuptake  
- Inhibitory (I): GABA, converted to succinate semialdehyde by GABA‑T, then oxidised to succinate (used in Krebs cycle)  

**4. Formation of GABA**  
- Derived from glutamic acid by glutamic acid decarboxylase, requires pyridoxal phosphate as coenzyme  

**5. Function, origin, and fate of glycine**  
- Major inhibitory neurotransmitter  
- Synthesised from serine via serine hydroxymethyltransferase  
- Removed by reuptake through high‑affinity transporters  

**6. Synthesis & degradation of acetylcholine (ACh)**  
- Synthesis: Acetyl CoA + choline —ChAT→ ACh + CoA  
- Degradation: ACh —AChE→ acetate + choline  

**7. Precursor of catecholamines and enzyme producing dopamine**  
- Precursor: Tyrosine  
- Enzyme: Dopa decarboxylase  

**8. Rate‑limiting step of catecholamine synthesis**  
- Tyrosine hydroxylase  

**9. Enzyme catalysing formation of NE and E**  
- NE: Dopamine β‑hydroxylase  
- E: Phenyl‑ethanolamine N‑methyltransferase  

**10. Fates of catecholamines**  
1. Active uptake by sympathetic nerve endings  
2. Catabolism by MAO & COMT:  
   - Dopamine → Homovanillic acid  
   - NE & E → Vanillylmandelic acid  

**11. Parkinson’s occurrence & lab finding**  
- Caused by idiopathic loss of dopamine‑producing cells → dopamine deficiency  
- Lab: Decreased HVA in urine  

**12. Drug used to treat Parkinson’s**  
- L‑DOPA  

**13. Justification of carbidopa addition to L‑DOPA**  
- Inhibits dopa‑decarboxylase → reduces peripheral L‑DOPA decarboxylation → more reaches brain  

**14. Alpha‑methyl DOPA mechanism**  
- Competitive inhibition of DOPA decarboxylase  

**15. Pheochromocytoma drug treatment & mechanism**  
- Drug: Alpha‑methyl tyrosine  
- Mechanism: Competes with tyrosine for tyrosine hydroxylase  

**16. MAOI mechanism & use**  
- Inactivates MAO → increases NE & serotonin levels  
- Use: Antidepressant  

**17. Functions of serotonin**  
- CNS: NT, mood, appetite, temperature regulation, melatonin precursor  
- Smooth muscle contraction (vasoconstriction)  
- GI motility  

**18. Serotonin synthesis & degradation**  
- Synthesis: Tryptophan → (tryptophan hydroxylase + BH₄) → 5‑hydroxytryptophan → decarboxylation → serotonin  
- Degradation: MAO → 5‑hydroxyindoleacetic acid  

**19. Carcinoid tumour**  
- Arises from argentaffin cells  
- ↑ Serotonin, ↓ Niacin → Pellagra  
- ↑ 5‑HIAA in urine  

**20. SSRI use**  
- Antidepressants  

**21. Functions of melatonin**  
- Stimulates immune system, antioxidant, regulates sleep  
- Follows circadian rhythm  

**22. Causes of melatonin deficiency**  
- Cigarettes, caffeine, stress  
- Antidepressants & beta‑blockers  
- Aging  

**23. Histamine synthesis enzyme**  
- Histidine decarboxylase  

**24. Functions of histamine**  
- Allergic & inflammatory reactions, gastric acid secretion  
- Arousal & wakefulness  
- Vasodilation  

**25. NO synthesis**  
- L‑arginine —(NO synthase)→ Nitric oxide  

**26. Physiological functions of NO**  
- Vasodilation: cerebral, coronary, muscular arteries  
- ↓ GI & urogenital motility  
- Hormone release from hypothalamus  
- Immune defence: macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils  

**27. Pharmacological functions of NO**  
- Nitroglycerin: angina  
- NO inhalation: pulmonary vasodilation, pulmonary HTN, high‑altitude pulmonary oedema  

**28. Steps of serotonin formation from tryptophan**  
1. Hydroxylation  
2. Decarboxylation  

**29. Steps of melatonin formation from serotonin**  
1. Acetylation  
2. Methylation

## Amino Acid Metabolism & Hyperammonemia

**1. Normal blood ammonia level**  
- 5–35 µmol/L  

**2. Major sources of ammonia**  
- Amino acids (most significant)  
  1. From glutamine  
  2. From bacteria  
  3. From purines & pyrimidines  
  4. From dietary or endogenous amines (hormones & neurotransmitters)  

**3. Mechanism by which glutamine produces ammonia**  
- Glutamine is released from branched‑chain amino acid breakdown in skeletal muscle  
- In liver: glutaminase & glutamate dehydrogenase produce ammonia → detoxified to urea  
- In kidney: excreted as NH₄⁺  

**4. Bacterial production of ammonia**  
- By urease  

**5. Phases of amino acid catabolism**  
1. Removal of α‑amino groups → ammonia + α‑keto acids  
2. Metabolism of α‑keto acids → energy intermediates (glucose, ketone bodies, fatty acids, CO₂, H₂O)  

**6. Deamination**  
- Removal of amino group from amino acid → ammonia + α‑keto acid  

**7. Enzymes of oxidative deamination**  
- Glutamate dehydrogenase  
- L‑amino acid oxidases  
- D‑amino acid oxidases  

**8. Glutamate dehydrogenase reaction**  
- Reversible oxidative deamination of glutamate → α‑ketoglutarate + ammonia  
- Requires NAD⁺ (oxidative) or NADP⁺ (reductive amination)  

**9. Regulation of glutamate dehydrogenase**  
- Inhibited by: ATP, GTP, NADH  
- Activated by: ADP, GDP  

**10. D‑amino acid oxidases**  
- Deaminate glycine  
- Require FAD as coenzyme  

**11. Importance of D‑amino acid oxidases**  
- Removes asymmetry → α‑keto acids can be aminated to L‑amino acids usable by the body  

**12. Deaminases**  
- Glutaminase & asparaginase  

**13. Coenzyme of transaminases**  
- Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)  

**14. Amino acids not undergoing transamination**  
- Lysine & threonine  
- Cyclic imino acids: proline & hydroxyproline  

**15. Transdeamination**  
- Coupling of transamination + oxidative deamination  
- Transamination collects amino groups in L‑glutamate → oxidative deamination in liver → ammonia release  

**16. Classification of amino acids by fate**  
- Glucogenic: pyruvate, oxaloacetate, or TCA intermediates → gluconeogenesis  
- Ketogenic: acetyl‑CoA, acetoacetate, acetoacetyl‑CoA → ketone bodies (lysine & leucine)  
- Mixed: both glucogenic & ketogenic (tyrosine, tryptophan, threonine, phenylalanine, isoleucine)  

**17. Fates of ammonia**  
- Biosynthesis: urea, non‑essential amino acids, monoamines, purines, pyrimidines  
- Small amounts excreted in urine  

**18. Pathophysiology of ammonia toxicity**  
- Excess ammonia → glutamate → glutamine via glutamine synthetase → depletes α‑ketoglutarate & GABA  
- Glutamine formation depletes ATP  
- ↑ permeability to K⁺ & Cl⁻  
- Excess glutamine exchanges for tryptophan → ↓ serotonin  
- Osmotic shift → cerebral oedema  

**19. Urea cycle location**  
- First 2 steps: mitochondria  
- Last 3 steps: cytosol  

**20. Energy consumption of urea cycle**  
- 3 ATP (4 high‑energy bonds)  

**21. Ammonia transport in circulation**  
- Glutamine & alanine  

**22. Steps of urea biosynthesis**  
1. Carbamoyl phosphate synthesis  
2. Citrulline synthesis  
3. Argininosuccinate synthesis  
4. Argininosuccinate cleavage  
5. Arginine cleavage → urea  

**23. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase‑1 (CPS1) reaction**  
- Uses ammonia, CO₂, phosphate, 2 ATP → carbamoyl phosphate  

**24. Ornithine transcarbamoylase forms**  
- Citrulline  

**25. Urea cycle enzyme converting ATP → AMP**  
- Argininosuccinate synthetase  

**26. Argininosuccinate lyase reaction**  
- Cleaves argininosuccinate → arginine + fumarate  
- Fumarate → oxaloacetate → aspartate regeneration  

**27. Arginase reaction**  
- Arginine → ornithine + urea  

**28. Sources of urea nitrogen**  
- Ammonia & aspartate  

**29. Short‑term regulation of urea cycle**  
- CPS1 allosterically activated by N‑acetylglutamate  
- N‑acetylglutamate synthase activated by arginine  

**30. Link between TCA & urea cycles**  
- CO₂ from TCA → carbamoyl phosphate  
- Ammonia from glutamate via GDH  
- ATP from TCA used in urea cycle  
- Fumarate from urea cycle → oxaloacetate → aspartate for urea cycle  

**31. Causes of congenital hyperammonemia**  
- Deficiency of any urea cycle enzyme  
- Defective membrane ornithine transporter  
- N‑acetylglutamate synthase deficiency  

**32. Lactulose in hepatic encephalopathy**  
- Acidifies gut → NH₃ → NH₄⁺ → excreted  
- Reduces ammoniagenic bacteria, increases lactobacilli  

**33. Phenylbutyrate in hepatic encephalopathy**  
- Converted to phenylacetate → binds glutamine → phenylacetylglutamine → urine excretion → ammonia lost  

**34. Sodium benzoate in hepatic encephalopathy**  
- Binds glycine → hippurate → excreted → ammonia lost  

**35. Hepa‑Merz in hepatic encephalopathy**  
- L‑ornithine activates urea cycle  
- L‑ornithine + L‑aspartate → glutamate → glutamine formation → ammonia detoxification  

**36. Tyrosine formation enzyme & coenzyme**  
- Enzyme: phenylalanine hydroxylase  
- Coenzyme: tetrahydrobiopterin  

**37. Clinical picture of phenylketonuria (PKU)**  
- Mental retardation (toxic products of phenylalanine)  
- Depigmentation (↓ melanin)  
- Mousy odour of urine  

**38. PKU treatment**  
- Low phenylalanine diet  
- Tyrosine & tetrahydrobiopterin supplementation  
- Early treatment prevents neurologic damage  

**39. Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD) enzyme deficiency**  
- Branched‑chain α‑ketoacid dehydrogenase complex  
- Catalyses oxidative decarboxylation of branched‑chain amino acids  

**40. Clinical picture of MSUD**  
- Mental retardation, early death  
- Sweet‑smelling urine (burnt sugar) due to ↑ isoleucine  

**41. MSUD treatment**  
- Diet low in branched‑chain amino acids

## Vitamin Deficiency & One Carbon Unit Metabolism

**1. Enzymes requiring thiamine & deficiency diseases**  
- Enzymes: Pyruvate dehydrogenase (oxidative decarboxylation), α‑ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, transketolase, branched‑chain ketoacid dehydrogenase  
- Deficiency diseases:  
  - Wernicke‑Korsakoff (psychosis & encephalopathy, acute in alcoholics)  
  - Beriberi (wet & dry forms)  

**2. Thiamine coenzyme form**  
- TPP (Thiamine pyrophosphate)  
- Formed by ATP‑dependent reaction in brain & liver  

**3. Tests for thiamine status**  
- Blood thiamine  
- Urine thiamine  
- Erythrocyte transketolase activity  
- Thiamine pyrophosphate effect  
- Blood pyruvate & lactate  

**4. Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) deficiency**  
- **Biochemical basis:** Pyruvate → acetyl‑CoA blocked → citrate production limited → TCA impaired → energy deficit  
- **Clinical picture:**  
  - Lactic acidosis (pyruvate diversion)  
  - Neurological: hypotonia, poor feeding, lethargy, mental retardation, microcephaly, blindness, spasticity  

**5. PDH deficiency treatment**  
- Dietary carbohydrate restriction  
- Correction of acidosis  

**6. Niacin chemical structure**  
- Pyridine‑3‑carboxylic acid  

**7. Sources of niacin**  
- Food: unrefined grains, milk, lean meat  
- Tryptophan  

**8. Therapeutic use of niacin**  
- Lowers plasma cholesterol & TAGs (inhibits adipose lipolysis → ↓ circulating FFAs)  

**9. Pellagra: symptoms & causes**  
- Symptoms: Dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia, death  
- Causes: Diet low in tryptophan & niacin, defective absorption (Hartnup disease), B6 deficiency, carcinoid tumour  

**10. Folic acid chemical composition**  
- Pteridine ring + p‑aminobenzoate (pABA) + glutamate  

**11. Folate activation reaction**  
- Two sequential NADPH‑dependent enzymes: folate reductase → dihydrofolate reductase  

**12. Folate‑targeting drugs**  
- Sulfa drugs: inhibit folic acid synthesis  
- Trimethoprim: inhibits dihydrofolate reductase  

**13. Metabolic role of tetrahydrofolate (THF)**  
- Carrier of one‑carbon units for synthesis of choline, serine, glycine, methionine, purines  

**14. Oxidation states of one‑carbon units**  
- Most reduced: methyl  
- Intermediate: methylene  
- Most oxidised: formyl, formimino, methenyl  

**15. Carriers of one‑carbon units**  
- Tetrahydrofolate (THF)  
- S‑adenosylmethionine (SAM)  

**16. CO₂ carrier**  
- Biotin  

**17. THF binding sites for one‑carbon units**  
- N5, N10, or both  

**18. Enzymes interconverting one‑carbon units**  
- NAD‑dependent dehydrogenase  
- NADP‑dependent reductase  

**19. Methylene group: sources & fate**  
- Sources: β‑carbon of serine, glycine (glycine cleavage system)  
- Fate: Oxidised → methenyl‑THF, reduced → methyl‑THF, used for thymidylate synthesis  

**20. Methenyl group: sources & fate**  
- Sources: Histidine → FIGLU → 5‑formimino‑THF → 5,10‑methenyl‑THF  
- Fate: Hydrolysed → N10‑formyl‑THF  

**21. Formyl group: sources & fate**  
- Sources: Activation of free formate (ATP + formyl‑THF synthetase), hydrolysis of methenyl‑THF, tryptophan catabolism  
- Fate: C2 & C8 in purines  

**22. Methyl‑THF: sources & fate**  
- Source: Reduction of methylene‑THF (methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase, NAD‑linked)  
- Fate: Transfers CH₃ to vitamin B₁₂ → methylcobalamin → methylation of homocysteine → methionine  

**23. Methyl trap**  
- Definition: B₁₂ deficiency → THF converted to methyl‑THF → cannot be metabolised → THF deficiency → functional folate deficiency

## Tetanus & Botulism

**1. Toxin of tetanus**  
- Tetanospasmin  

**2. Transport & antigenic type**  
- Retrograde axonal transport; one antigenic type  

**3. Clinical picture**  
- Muscle spasms, trismus, risus sardonicus, exaggerated reflexes, respiratory failure  

**4. Cultivation of C. tetani**  
- Robertson cooked meat medium → subculture on blood agar anaerobically 2–3 days at 37°C  

**5. Antibiotics**  
- Metronidazole or penicillin G  

**6. Wound management (gross contamination)**  
- Tetanus IG + toxoid booster + penicillin  

**7. Botulinum toxin**  
- Blocks ACh release; encoded by lysogenic phage; types A, B, E cause human illness  

**8. Clinical picture**  
- Descending paralysis, afebrile; wound botulism, infant botulism  

**9. Cultivation & biochemical testing**  
- Robertson cooked meat medium → blood agar anaerobically 37°C/48 hrs; ferments glucose & maltose; proteolytic strains blacken medium, gelatin liquefaction, H₂S production  

**10. Prevention**  
- Proper canning & cooking; discard swollen cans

## Meningitis Overview

**11. Routes of CNS infection**  
- Haematogenous (arterial/retrograde venous), direct trauma/lumbar puncture, local extension (skull/spine), peripheral nerves  

**12. Classification**

# HIGH‑YIELD NEUROSCIENCE & METABOLISM REVIEW SHEET

## 1. Neurotransmitters & Brain Chemistry

- **Classification:** Structural – amino acids, biogenic amines, neuropeptides, others; Functional – excitatory, inhibitory, or both  
- **Inactivation & therapeutic applications:** Reuptake → SSRIs; Hydrolysis → MAO inhibitors  
- **Principal NTs:** Excitatory: Glutamate → reuptake; Inhibitory: GABA → converted to succinate semialdehyde by GABA‑T → succinate (Krebs cycle)  
- **GABA Formation:** Glutamic acid → GABA via glutamic acid decarboxylase (requires pyridoxal phosphate)  
- **Glycine:** Major inhibitory NT; synthesis from serine via serine hydroxymethyltransferase; removal by reuptake via high‑affinity transporters  
- **Acetylcholine (ACh):** Synthesis: Acetyl‑CoA + Choline —ChAT→ ACh + CoA; Degradation: ACh —AChE→ acetate + choline  
- **Catecholamines:** Precursor: Tyrosine → dopamine (via DOPA decarboxylase); Rate‑limiting step: Tyrosine hydroxylase; NE formation: Dopamine β‑hydroxylase; E formation: Phenylethanolamine N‑methyltransferase; Fate: uptake, catabolism (MAO & COMT), D → homovanillic acid, NE/E → VMA  
- **Serotonin (5‑HT):** Synthesis: Tryptophan → 5‑hydroxytryptophan → serotonin; Degradation: MAO → 5‑HIAA; Functions: mood, appetite, temp regulation, GIT motility, precursor for melatonin  
- **Melatonin:** Functions: sleep regulation, antioxidant, immune stimulation; Deficiency: cigarettes, caffeine, stress, antidepressants, beta‑blockers, aging  
- **Histamine:** Synthesis: Histidine → histamine via histidine decarboxylase; Functions: allergic/inflammatory reactions, gastric acid secretion, arousal, vasodilation  
- **Nitric Oxide (NO):** Synthesis: L‑arginine → NO via nitric oxide synthase; Functions: vasodilation, hormone release, immune defence; Pharmacological: nitroglycerin (angina), NO inhalation (pulmonary hypertension)  
- **Parkinson’s Disease:** Cause: loss of dopamine‑producing cells; Lab: ↓ HVA in urine; Treatment: L‑DOPA ± carbidopa (prevents peripheral decarboxylation)  
- **Pheochromocytoma treatment:** Alpha‑methyl tyrosine → inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase  
- **MAO inhibitors:** Increase NE & serotonin → antidepressant

## 2. Brain Energy Metabolism

- **Brain fuel:** Glucose, ketone bodies, monocarboxylic acids  
- **Gluconeogenesis carbon sources:** Lactate, amino acids, glycerol  
- **Glucose phosphorylation:** Hexokinase  
- **Glucose uptake:** Astrocytes → GLUT1; Neurons → GLUT3  
- **Astrocyte‑neuron lactate shuttle:** Bypasses slower neuronal glycolysis (PFK‑1 regulation)  
- **Lactate transport:** Monocarboxylate transporters  
- **Glycogen regulation:** Neurotransmitters + local energy status  
- **Pentose phosphate pathway:** Maintains glutathione for ROS detoxification  
- **Importance of brain energy metabolism:** Maintains ion gradients, neurotransmitter recycling; diet affects function

## 3. Addiction & Reward Pathways

- **Effect of drugs:** Heroin, alcohol, cannabis, tobacco → VTA → NAc → DA → euphoria; Alcohol → endogenous opioids; BZDs → stimulate DA in VTA; Amphetamine, cocaine → inhibit DA reuptake in NAc  
- **Reward deficiency & withdrawal:** Chronic drug use → dopaminergic degeneration → craving, anhedonia; Withdrawal → CRF & NA neurons in amygdala → sympathetic overactivity  
- **Management:**  
  - BZD/barbiturates: replace short‑acting with long‑acting  
  - Alcohol: Diazepam/chlordiazepoxide, thiamine, disulfiram, naltrexone, acamprosate  
  - Opioids: Methadone/buprenorphine, naltrexone, symptomatic meds (clonidine, antiemetics)  
  - Nicotine: psychotherapy, nicotine replacement, bupropion, varenicline  
  - LSD: calm patient, BZD/antipsychotics  
  - Cannabis: naltrexone, buspirone, fluoxetine, dronabinol  
- **Cocaine:** IV → intense euphoria, infection risk; Intranasal → less euphoria, nasal septum perforation

## 4. Ammonia & Urea Metabolism

- **Normal blood ammonia:** 5–35 μmol/L  
- **Sources:** Glutamine, bacteria (urease), amino acids, purines/pyrimidines, dietary amines  
- **Glutamine → ammonia:** Muscle → liver (glutaminase & GDH), kidney excretes NH₄⁺, liver → urea  
- **Deamination:** Removal of amino group → ammonia + α‑keto acid  
- **Oxidative deamination enzymes:** GDH, L‑amino acid oxidases, D‑amino acid oxidases  
- **GDH regulation:** Activated by ADP, GDP; Inhibited by ATP, GTP, NADH  
- **Transamination:** Requires PLP  
- **Transdeamination:** Glutamate collects amino groups → oxidative deamination → ammonia  
- **Amino acid classification:** Glucogenic → pyruvate, oxaloacetate, TCA intermediates; Ketogenic → acetyl‑CoA, acetoacetate (lysine, leucine); Mixed → tyrosine, tryptophan, threonine, phenylalanine, isoleucine  
- **Urea cycle:** First 2 steps: mitochondria; last 3: cytosol; Energy: 3 ATP → 4 high‑energy bonds; Ammonia transport: glutamine, alanine; Key enzymes: CPS1, OTC, ASS, ASL, arginase; Regulation: CPS1 activated by N‑acetylglutamate (arginine allosteric activator)  
- **Congenital hyperammonemia:** enzyme deficiencies, membrane transporter defects, N‑acetylglutamate synthase deficiency  
- **Hepatic encephalopathy treatments:** Lactulose → acidifies gut, traps NH₃ as NH₄⁺; Phenylbutyrate → phenylacetylglutamine excretion; Sodium benzoate → hippurate excretion; Hepa‑Merz → L‑ornithine activates urea cycle, transfers ammonia to glutamine

## 5. Amino Acid & Vitamin Metabolism

- **Tyrosine formation:** Phenylalanine hydroxylase + tetrahydrobiopterin  
- **Phenylketonuria:** Mental retardation, depigmentation, mousy odour; treat: low Phe diet, tyrosine + BH₄ supplement  
- **Maple syrup urine disease:** Deficient branched‑chain α‑ketoacid dehydrogenase → sweet‑smelling urine, neurological damage; treat: low branched‑chain AA diet  
- **Thiamine:** Enzymes: PDH, α‑KGDH, transketolase, BCKD; Deficiency: Wernicke‑Korsakoff, beriberi; Tests: blood/urine thiamine, RBC transketolase, TPP effect, blood pyruvate/lactate  
- **Niacin (B3):** Pyridine‑3‑carboxylic acid; lowers cholesterol/TAGs; sources: tryptophan, grains, meat  
- **Pellagra:** Dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia, death  
- **Folate:** Pteridine + pABA + glutamate; Activated by folate reductase & dihydrofolate reductase; One‑carbon carrier (THF, SAM); B₁₂ deficiency → methyl trap

- **Tetanus:** Toxin tetanospasmin, retrograde transport; Clinical: muscle spasms, trismus, risus sardonicus, respiratory failure; Culture: Robertson meat → blood agar anaerobic 37°C; Treatment: metronidazole/penicillin; wound → tetanus IG + toxoid booster

# CNS Physiology

## Neurotransmitters

**1. Define neuromodulators, giving examples**  
- They are synapse‑associated chemical substances that affect the activity of several neurons. They have more time affecting the synapses before being degraded; differ from transmitters with regards duration of action.  
- Examples: Adenosine, ATP, NO  

**2. Define neurohormones, giving examples**  
- Neurohormones are chemical substances synthesised in neurons and secreted directly into the bloodstream.  
- Examples: Oxytocin and vasopressin (from hypothalamus)  

**3. Source and functions of acetylcholine (ACh)**  
- **Source:** motor neurons, basal ganglia, preganglionic autonomic neurons, postganglionic parasympathetic neurons, sympathetic neurons supplying sweat glands and skeletal muscle blood vessels, highest concentration in motor cortex and thalamus  
- **Functions:** arousal, learning, motor control  

**4. Source and functions of norepinephrine (NE)**  
- **Source:** pons and medulla, most postganglionic sympathetic neurons  
- **Functions:** control of mood, reward system, arousal, regulation of feeding, regulation of blood pressure, inhibitory via β₂ receptors and excitatory via α₁ receptors  

**5. Neurotransmitter representing more than 50% of CNS catecholamines and its sites**  
- Dopamine  
- Sites: Basal ganglia and limbic system  

**6. State the primary mechanism of cocaine**  
- Cocaine binds to reuptake transporters of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, blocking their reuptake into the presynaptic nerve endings.  

**7. Mechanism of action of GABA**  
- GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that increases chloride ion (Cl⁻) conductance, leading to membrane hyperpolarisation.  

**8. Write a short note on benzodiazepines**  
- Benzodiazepines are drugs that increase the ability of GABA to activate its receptors in the brain and spinal cord. They are used in: epileptic seizures, seizures due to drug overdose or poisoning, anxiety disorders, insomnia.  

**9. Glycine is found in …… & …… and is antagonised by ……**  
- Found in: Spinal cord and medulla  
- Antagonised by: Strychnine  

**10. Mention glutamate and aspartate receptors and their functions**  
- **Receptors:** NMDA, AMPA, Kainate  
- **Functions:** memory, learning  

**11. Source and function of Substance P**  
- **Source:** present in the endings of primary sensory neurons in the spinal cord and brainstem  
- **Function:** excitatory neurotransmitter mediating pain sensation  

**12. Slowly acting neurotransmitters are …… & ……**  
- Substance P  
- Endogenous opioid peptides

## Sensory Receptors & Sensory Coding

**1. Mention the functions of sensory receptors**  
- Detect stimuli  
- Act as transducers producing generator potentials  
- Generate action potentials in afferent nerves  
- Inform the CNS about changes in the internal and external environment  

**2. Mention the properties of sensory receptors and the corresponding laws (if present)**  
- Specificity – Müller’s law  
- Excitability – generation of receptor (generator) potential  
- Discharge of impulses – Weber–Fechner law  
- Adaptation  

**3. State Weber–Fechner law**  
- The receptor generates action potentials in afferent neurons at a frequency proportional to the logarithm of stimulus intensity.  

**4. Mention the mechanism of receptor adaptation**  
- Readjustment of the receptor due to redistribution of fluid, so receptor potential is no longer elicited  
- Accommodation due to progressive inactivation of sodium (Na⁺) channels  

**5. Describe the mechanism of modality discrimination**  
- Type of receptor stimulated  
- Specific sensory pathway activated  
- Labelled‑line principle  

**6. …… helps the CNS to localise a stimulus**  
- Lateral inhibition  

**7. Explain the phantom limb phenomenon**  
- After amputation, nerves remaining in the stump form nodules called neuromas, which generate impulses interpreted by the brain as originating from the missing limb.  

**8. Mention the factors involved in discrimination of:**  
- **a) Intensity:** frequency of action potentials, number of receptors stimulated  
- **b) Duration:** ability of receptors to adapt slowly or rapidly  

**9. Classify receptors according to adaptation, giving examples**  
- Rapidly adapting: touch receptors  
- Moderately adapting: thermoreceptors  
- Slowly adapting: pain receptors

## Pain And Hyperalgesia

**1. Mention areas with no nociceptors**  
- Brain, bone, liver parenchyma, lung alveoli.  

**2. Describe the mechanism of stimulation of nociceptors**  
- Tissue damage leads to accumulation of pain‑producing substances (kinins, potassium, histamine, substance P). These stimulate chemosensitive pain receptors and lower the threshold of thermosensitive and mechanosensitive pain receptors.  

**3. Higher nervous perception site of pain and its adaptability**  
- Thalamus and sensory cortex.  
- Pain receptors are non‑adapting (tonic receptors).  

**4. Causes of cutaneous pain**  
- Injury or inflammation of the skin  
- Irritation of dorsal roots  
- Referred pain from diseased deep or visceral structures  

**5. Compare fast and slow cutaneous pain with respect to summation, receptors, and perception**  
- **Fast pain:** no summation; mechanosensitive and thermosensitive receptors; perceived in sensory cortex  
- **Slow pain:** summation present; mainly chemosensitive receptors; perceived in reticular formation, specific thalamic nuclei, and tectum of midbrain  

**6. Fast pain fibres terminate on which structures?**  
- In spinal cord: lamina marginalis  
- In thalamus: ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL)  

**7. Slow pain fibres end on which laminae in the spinal cord?**  
- Lamina II and III (substantia gelatinosa).  

**8. Enumerate structures where most neurons of the paleospinothalamic pathway end**  
- Reticular nuclei of brainstem  
- Tectal area of midbrain  
- Periaqueductal gray region  

**9. Causes of deep pain**  
- Injury or inflammation of deep structures  
- Ischaemia or spasm of muscles  

**10. Causes of visceral pain**  
- Inflammation of peritoneal covering of viscera  
- Irritation or ischaemia of viscera  
- Spasm or overdistension of viscera  

**11. Describe characters of visceral pain**  
- Same characters as slow cutaneous pain  
- May be associated with depressor autonomic effects  
- Rigidity of overlying skeletal muscles  
- Referred pain to other structures  

**12. Compare slow and fast pain regarding somatic, autonomic, and emotional reactions**  
- **Fast pain:** flexor withdrawal reflex, sympathetic activity, crying, restlessness, anxiety  
- **Slow pain:** rigidity of overlying muscles (deep and visceral pain), parasympathetic effects, emotional depression and sadness  

**13. Secondary hyperalgesia frequently results from:**  
- Lesions in the spinal cord or thalamus.  

**14. Compare primary and secondary hyperalgesia**  
- **Primary hyperalgesia:** occurs in inflamed skin around injury; due to chemical facilitation of pain receptors (e.g., substance P); non‑painful stimulus becomes painful  
- **Secondary hyperalgesia:** occurs in healthy skin; explained by convergence‑facilitation theory; painful stimulus becomes more painful  

**15. Clinical picture of herpes zoster**  
- Severe pain followed within a few days by a unilateral rash limited to the dermatome supplied by the affected dorsal root ganglion.  

**16. Tic douloureux is:**  
- Trigeminal neuralgia (fifth cranial nerve).  

**17. Give examples of referred pain**  
- Gall bladder → epigastrium and tip of right scapula  
- Appendix → periumbilical region  
- Kidney → back, inguinal region, testis  
- Heart → inner aspect of left arm  

**18. Mention the dermatomal rule with examples**  
- Pain is referred to dermatomes sharing the same embryonic segment as the painful structure.  
- Example: Heart and arm share embryonic origin; testes migrate with nerves from primitive urogenital ridge.  

**19. Compare convergence‑projection and facilitation theories**  
- **Convergence‑projection theory:** visceral and cutaneous afferents converge on the same second‑order neuron, and the brain projects pain to the skin.  
- **Facilitation theory:** visceral and cutaneous afferents converge on the same spinal segment but different second‑order neurons; visceral input facilitates skin pathways.  

**20. Mention extracranial causes of headache**  
- Spasm of head and neck muscles  
- Nasal irritation  
- Ear disorders  
- Eye disorders and refractive errors  
- Dental disorders  

**21. Mention intracranial pain‑sensitive structures**  
- Venous sinuses, tentorium, dura at the base of the brain, and meningeal blood vessels (especially middle meningeal arteries).  

**22. Headache due to intracranial causes is referred to:**  
- Frontal half of head (above tentorium)  
- Occipital region (below tentorium)  

**23. List intracranial causes of headache**  
- Meningeal irritation  
- Low CSF pressure  
- Alcohol  
- Hypertension  
- Migraine  

**24. Describe steps of the pain analgesia system in brain and spinal cord**  
- The periventricular area of the hypothalamus releases β‑endorphins → stimulates periaqueductal gray → releases enkephalins → stimulates nucleus raphe magnus → releases serotonin → activates inhibitory interneurons in dorsal horn → interneurons release enkephalins causing pre‑ and postsynaptic inhibition of pain fibres.  

**25. Mention components of the brain opiate system and their sites**  
- β‑endorphin: hypothalamus and pituitary  
- Met‑ and leu‑enkephalins: brainstem and spinal cord  
- Dynorphin: same areas as enkephalins  

**26. Explain the gate theory of pain**  
- The substantia gelatinosa acts as a gate for pain transmission. Pain inhibition occurs via lateral inhibition through interneurons releasing GABA, enkephalins, or serotonin.  

**27. Mention sites of opioid receptors**  
- Periventricular hypothalamus  
- Periaqueductal gray area  
- Raphe magnus nucleus  

**28. Mention sites where electrical stimulation produces analgesia**  
- Periaqueductal gray  
- Raphe magnus nucleus  
- Periventricular nuclei  
- Areas stimulating large sensory fibres  

**29. Mention mechanisms of surgical pain control**  
- Cordotomy (interruption of spinothalamic tract)  
- Cauterisation of specific pain areas in intralaminar thalamic nuclei

**1. S‑II receives fibres from:**

**18. Uncrossed corticospinal fibres supply:**

**19. Parts of corticobulbar tract receiving only contralateral fibres**

**20. Compare pyramidal and extrapyramidal tracts**

**21. Compare tectospinal and rubrospinal tracts**

## Muscle Stretch Reflex

**1. Define reflex arc and mention its components**  
- It is the basic functional unit of the nervous system, being the smallest and simplest unit capable of receiving a stimulus and producing a response.  
- Components: receptor, sensory neuron, integrating centre, motor neuron, effector organ.  

**2. Compare afferent and efferent fibres of the myotactic reflex**  
- Afferent: Type Ia rapidly conducting fibres and type II fibres.  
- Efferent: Alpha motor neurons from the spinal cord.  

**3. List the clinical importance of the muscle spindle reflex**  
- Examination of static (muscle tone) and dynamic (deep reflexes) stretch reflexes  
- Diagnosis of the site and cause of neurological diseases  

**4. Compare nuclear bag and nuclear chain fibres with respect to function**  
- **Nuclear bag fibres:** detect rate of change of muscle length (dynamic response); phasic receptors (stop discharge during maintained stretch)  
- **Nuclear chain fibres:** detect degree of change of muscle length (static response); tonic receptors (continue discharge during maintained stretch)  

**5. Compare primary and secondary afferent endings in myotactic reflex**  
- **Primary endings:** annulospiral endings; type Ia fibres; around central part of nuclear bag and chain; rapid conduction; respond to dynamic and static stimuli  
- **Secondary endings:** flower‑spray endings; type II fibres; around nuclear chain only; slow conduction; respond to static stimuli only  

**6. Describe efferent innervation of the myotactic reflex**  
- Alpha motor neurons: innervate extrafusal muscle fibres  
- Gamma motor neurons: dynamic gamma to nuclear bag fibres; static gamma to nuclear chain fibres  

**7. Mention causes of stimulation of intrafusal fibres**  
- Stretch of the central non‑contractile part due to:  
  1. Lengthening of the whole skeletal muscle  
  2. Contraction of peripheral contractile parts by gamma efferents  
- Stimulation is strongest when both occur together.  

**8. Synaptic delay of the myotactic reflex is:**  
- 0.3–0.5 milliseconds.  

**9. Mention inhibitors of the stretch reflex**  
- Active muscle contraction (silent period of muscle spindle)  
- Stretch reflex of antagonist muscle  
- Golgi tendon organ reflex (inverse stretch reflex)  

**10. Compare static and dynamic stretch reflexes with respect to stimulus and receptor**  
- **Static stretch reflex:** stimulus: maintained weak stretch; receptor: nuclear chain fibres (slowly adapting)  
- **Dynamic stretch reflex:** stimulus: sudden rapid stretch; receptor: nuclear bag fibres (rapidly adapting)  

**11. Explain alpha–gamma co‑activation**  
- During voluntary contraction, supraspinal centres activate both alpha and gamma motor neurons simultaneously.  
- Alpha motor neurons contract extrafusal fibres; gamma motor neurons contract intrafusal fibres to maintain spindle sensitivity.  
- If muscle load increases, stretch of intrafusal fibres increases Ia discharge, leading to increased alpha motor neuron activity and stronger contraction.  

**12. What happens if muscle spindle or its afferent or efferent fibres malfunction during voluntary movement?**  
- Muscle contraction becomes jerky and uncoordinated.  

**13. Describe the clinical value of the stretch reflex**  
- Presence of normal reflex indicates integrity of all components  
- Reflects balance between excitatory and inhibitory supraspinal influences  
- Helps localise neurological lesions  
- Hyperreflexia is associated with hypertonia and vice versa  

**14. Compare facilitatory and inhibitory centres affecting stretch reflex**  
- **Facilitatory centres:** motor area 4, caudate nucleus; act via pontine reticular formation (has intrinsic activity); vestibular nuclei via vestibulospinal tract  
- **Inhibitory centres:** cortical suppressor areas 4s and 6s, lentiform nucleus; act via medullary reticular formation (no intrinsic activity); red nucleus via rubrospinal tract

## Other Reflexes, Motor Hierarchy And Motor Cortex

**1. What is the stimulus of the Golgi tendon organ (GTO) reflex?**  
- Increase in active tension due to isometric muscle contraction  
- Increase in passive tension due to excessive stretch beyond physiological limits  

**2. GTO is arranged in ….. to extrafusal fibres and acts as ….. detectors**  
- Series  
- Tension detectors  

**3. The afferent of the GTO reflex is ….. which synapses with ….. in the spinal cord**  
- Ib fibres  
- Inhibitory interneurons  

**4. Mention the functions of the inverse stretch reflex**  
- Protects muscles and tendons from damage due to excessive tension  
- Equalises contractile force among muscle fibres; fibres producing excessive tension are inhibited  

**5. Golgi tendon organ reflex is ….. synaptic**  
- Bisynaptic  

**6. Mention the afferents of the flexor withdrawal reflex**  
- A‑delta fibres  
- C‑fiber pain afferents  

**7. GR: Stepping on a sharp object can cause hip flexion**  
- Due to discharge of impulses in many afferent fibres that spread excitatory impulses up and down several spinal segments, producing a greater response. This phenomenon is called irradiation.  

**8. ….. describes the property responsible for prolonged after‑discharge in the withdrawal reflex**  
- Reverberating circuits  

**9. ….. helps unopposed action of the agonist muscle in the withdrawal reflex**  
- Reciprocal innervation  

**10. Define the extensor (crossed extensor) reflex**  
- It is reflex extension of the limb in response to a painful stimulus applied to the opposite limb.  

**11. Afferents of the extensor reflex synapse with …..**  
- Intersegmental neurons  

**12. In contrast to superficial reflexes, deep reflexes do NOT require intact …..**  
- Upper motor neurons  

**13. The centre for superficial abdominal reflexes is ….. for epigastric reflexes and ….. for abdominal reflexes**  
- T6–T9  
- T9–T12  

**14. Mention physiological and pathological conditions of a positive Babinski sign**  
- **Physiological:** deep sleep, general anaesthesia, infants below 1 year (pyramidal tract not yet myelinated)  
- **Pathological:** deep coma, upper motor neuron lesion; fanning of toes (area 6 lesion); dorsiflexion of big toe (area 4 lesion)  

**15. Define motor neuron pool**

**16. Mention input sources to alpha motor neurons**  
- Sensory neurons from dorsal root ganglia  
- Interneurons  
- Descending projections from higher brain centres  

**17. The neurotransmitter of Renshaw cells is ….. and they are activated by …..**  
- Glycine  
- Collaterals from active alpha motor neurons releasing acetylcholine  

**18. Describe the actions of Renshaw cells**  
- Provide negative feedback to active alpha motor neurons  
- Inhibit surrounding alpha motor neurons to focus motor activity  
- Prevent oscillation by inhibiting antagonist muscles  

**19. Lesion of ….. causes loss of fine movements, while lesion of ….. causes weakness of gross movements**  
- Area 4  
- Area 4 or area 6  

**20. In voluntary movement, the idea is initiated in ….. and converted into an initial decision in …..**  
- Prefrontal cortex  
- Limbic association areas  

**21. ….. evaluates body position using somatosensory, proprioceptive, and visual inputs**  
- Posterior association area (areas 5 and 7)  

**22. ….. and ….. decide which muscle groups contract and the sequence of contraction**  
- Premotor area  
- Supplementary motor area  

**23. ….. and ….. share in planning movements by sequencing and storage**  
- Basal ganglia  
- Neocerebellum  

**24. Mention parts bilaterally represented in area 4**  
- Respiratory muscles  
- Abdominal muscles  
- Head muscles (except lower face and genioglossus)  

**25. Mention the part not inverted in motor representation**  
- Head  

**26. List the connections of area 4**  
- Anterior horn cells via corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts  
- Area 6 (premotor area)  
- Contralateral neocerebellum  
- Basal ganglia  

**27. Compare dynamic and static Betz cells**  
- **Dynamic Betz cells:** discharge at the start of voluntary movement; initiate and direct movement  
- **Static Betz cells:** discharge throughout movement; maintain muscle contraction  

**28. Mention functions of area 4**  
- Initiates fine skilled movements of distal muscles  
- Shares in gross proximal movements with area 6  
- Facilitates stretch reflex  
- Necessary for superficial reflexes and normal plantar response  

**29. Describe lesion of area 4**  
- Loss of fine voluntary movements of one limb  
- Paresis of gross movements

**30. Describe connections of area 6**

- Primary motor cortex  
- Basal ganglia  
- Cerebellum  
- Supplementary motor area

**31. Mention functions of area 6**  
- Stores motor plans for skilled movements  
- Adjusts posture for fine movements  
- Initiates gross proximal movements  
- Inhibits grasp reflex  
- Inhibits stretch reflex  
- Regulates automatic associated movements with basal ganglia  

**32. Describe effects of lesion of area 6**  
- Paresis  
- Motor apraxia  
- Return of grasp reflex  
- Hypertonia and hyperreflexia  
- Positive Babinski sign with toe fanning  
- Loss of automatic associated movements  

**33. Mention function and lesion of the hand skills area**  
- **Function:** controls complex skilled hand movements  
- **Lesion:** agraphia; inability to write or draw  

**34. Mention function and lesion of the eye movement area**  
- **Function:** voluntary conjugate deviation of eyes to the opposite side

**35. Mention function and lesion of the motor speech area**  
- **Function:** stores complex vocalisation programs  
- **Lesion:** motor aphasia  

**36. Describe body representation in medial area 6**  
- Horizontal and bilateral representation of body muscles  

**37. Mention functions of medial area 6**  
- Controls proximal muscles for internally directed movements  
- Plans complex bilateral movements before initiation  

**38. Describe effects of lesion of medial area 6**  
- Inability to perform complex movements  
- Poor coordination during bilateral hand movements  

**39. Cerebral capillaries are ….. except in …..**  
- Non‑fenestrated  
- Choroid plexus  

**40. Blood supply of brain is ….. and oxygen consumption is …..**  
- Blood flow: 750 mL/min (≈14% of cardiac output)  
- Oxygen consumption: 46 mL/min (≈18% of total body O₂)  

**41. Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) equals …..**  
- CPP = MAP − ICP  

**42. Autoregulation of cerebral blood flow maintains constant flow at MAP of …..**  
- 50–150 mmHg  

**43. Mention explanations of cerebral autoregulation**  
- Myogenic response of cerebral vessels  
- Metabolic regulation  

**44. Define Monro–Kellie doctrine**  
- The total volume of brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid within the rigid skull remains constant; therefore, an increase in one component must be compensated by a decrease in another. Increased ICP decreases cerebral blood flow and vice versa.

## Functions Of Descending Motor Tract

**1. GR: Why is it called the pyramidal tract?**  
- Because many fibres arise from the pyramidal (Betz) cells located in the primary motor cortex (area 4).  

**2. Mention the functions of the pyramidal tract**  
- Controls fine, skilled voluntary movements, especially at distal joints  
- Shares with extrapyramidal tracts in gross movements and postural adjustment as a background for fine movements  
- Facilitatory to superficial and deep reflexes  
- Inhibits primitive withdrawal reflexes  
- Corticobulbar component controls voluntary lateral eye movements  

**3. Mention the parts of the medial brainstem motor pathways**  
- Pathways working via vertical corticospinal tracts:  
  - Pontine reticulospinal (PRS)  
  - Medullary reticulospinal (MRS)  
  - Vestibulospinal tract  
  - Tectospinal tract  

**4. Describe the termination of medial brainstem pathways**  
- Terminate on interneurons in the anterior horn of the spinal cord to control axial and proximal muscles  
- Some fibres terminate directly on motor neurons controlling axial muscles  

**5. Describe the lateral brainstem pathway**  
- Arises from the red nucleus of the midbrain  
- Has somatotopic representation similar to area 4 but less precise  
- Terminates on interneurons in the ventral horn of the spinal cord  
- Controls distal muscles  
- Excites flexor muscles and inhibits extensor muscles  
- Acts as a weak alternative for fine skilled movements in pyramidal tract lesions  

**6. Enumerate the functions of the extrapyramidal tracts**  
- Control gross movements of axial and proximal muscles  
- Provide postural background necessary for fine skilled movements  
- Participate with basal ganglia in semiautomatic movements  
- In selective corticospinal tract lesions, rubrospinal tract can partially control hand and wrist movements (not fingers)  
- Regulation of muscle tone  
- Medial tectospinal tract: reflex head turning in response to sound  
- Lateral tectospinal tract: protective eye reflexes and head turning to visual stimuli  
- Vestibulospinal tract: activation of antigravity muscles  
- Autonomic motor functions  

**7. Describe the autonomic functions of extrapyramidal tracts**  
- Respiratory control: from respiratory centres to anterior horn cells of respiratory muscles  
- Vasomotor control: projections to lateral horn cells in thoracolumbar spinal cord  
- Sweat secretion: via hypothalamic heat‑regulating centres  

**8. Compare pyramidal and extrapyramidal tracts with respect to termination and time of functional onset**  
- **Pyramidal tract:** terminates on cranial nerve motor nuclei and alpha & gamma anterior horn cells; becomes functional after about one year of age  
- **Extrapyramidal tract:** terminates mainly on alpha & gamma motor neurons; functional during the first year of life  

**9. Mention the site of termination of medial and lateral motor systems in the spinal cord**  
- Medial motor system: medial (ventral) portion of the anterior horn  
- Lateral motor system: lateral portion of the anterior horn

## Sensory & Motor Systems Lesions

**1. Describe the effect of UMN lesion on muscle state**  
- Minimal muscle atrophy (disuse atrophy) due to loss of voluntary and reflex muscle activity; no loss of trophic effect on muscle metabolism because nerve supply remains intact  

**2. Compare UMN and LMN lesions with respect to reflexes**  
- **UMN lesion:** superficial reflexes lost, plantar reflex becomes positive (Babinski sign), deep reflexes exaggerated (hyperreflexia)  
- **LMN lesion:** all superficial reflexes lost, deep reflexes decreased or absent (hyporeflexia)  

**3. Mention distinguishing features of acute UMN lesion from LMN lesion**  
- Widespread paralysis, positive Babinski sign, no muscle atrophy  

**4. Compare corticobulbar lesion and facial nerve lesion**  
- **Corticobulbar lesion:** upper face spared, lower face affected, paralysis contralateral  
- **Facial nerve lesion:** both upper and lower face affected, paralysis ipsilateral  

**5. Mention acquired causes of polyneuropathy**  
- Metabolic (e.g., diabetes mellitus), autoimmune disorders, toxins, tumours, trauma, nutritional deficiency, alcoholism, vascular causes  

**6. Describe the sensory and motor effects of polyneuropathy**  
- **Sensory:** numbness, tingling, pricking sensation → progresses to hypesthesia then anesthesia; glove and stocking pattern of sensory loss  
- **Motor:** paresis or paralysis, loss of reflexes, muscle atrophy (LMN type)  

**7. Define syringomyelia**  
- A condition characterised by formation of a cyst, glial tissue, or fluid‑filled cavity around the central canal of the spinal cord, commonly in cervical segments, which may extend to thoracic segments and medulla.  

**8. Describe the effect of a small lesion in syringomyelia**  
- Damage to decussating fibres of the spinothalamic tract  
- Loss of pain and temperature sensation in upper limbs and upper trunk (jacket distribution)  
- Crude touch and dorsal column sensations preserved  

**9. Describe the effect of a progressive lesion in syringomyelia**  
- Sensory loss as in small lesion  
- LMN signs at the level of lesion due to anterior horn cell damage  
- UMN signs below the lesion due to corticospinal tract damage  
- Autonomic disturbance due to lateral horn cell damage  
- Horner’s syndrome  

**10. Describe the effect of a more advanced lesion in syringomyelia**  
- Sensory, motor, and autonomic effects as above  
- Involvement of cranial nerve nuclei (syringobulbia)  
- Brainstem affection causing facial sensory and motor deficits  

**11. Tabes dorsalis commonly affects which spinal segments?**  
- Lumbosacral segments  

**12. Mention the clinical picture of late‑stage syphilis (tabes dorsalis)**  
- Sensory loss, sensory ataxia, stamping gait, motor effects due to deafferentation (hypotonia and hyporeflexia)  

**13. Which sensation is first and last lost in tabes dorsalis, and why?**  
- First lost: vibration sense; last lost: slow pain; reason: lesion compresses from inside outward; pain fibres are centrally located  

**14. Define stamping gait**  
- A gait in which the patient lifts the legs high and strikes the ground forcefully while watching the feet due to loss of proprioception.  

**15. Define Argyll Robertson pupil**  
- Loss of pupillary reaction to light with preserved near response, seen in advanced tabes dorsalis due to damage of the pretectal area of the midbrain.  

**16. Describe the effects of Brown–Séquard syndrome**  
- **At the level of lesion:** LMN paralysis, loss of all sensations  
- **Below the level of lesion (ipsilateral):** UMN signs, loss of dorsal column sensations  
- **Below the level of lesion (contralateral):** loss of pain and temperature sensations  

**17. Mention causes of spinal cord transection**  
- Gunshot injuries, vertebral dislocation, vascular accidents  

**18. GR: Why does transection of the spinal cord above C5 lead to death?**  
- Because impulses from the respiratory centre cannot reach C3–C5 segments (phrenic nerve origin), leading to respiratory failure.  

**19. In spinal cord transection below C5, respiration is ______, while in mid‑thoracic transection it is ______**  
- Below C5: diaphragmatic only  
- Mid‑thoracic: diaphragmatic and intercostal  

**20. Describe the cause and duration of spinal shock**  
- **Cause:** sudden withdrawal of facilitatory impulses from corticospinal, reticulospinal, and vestibulospinal tracts; leads to decreased excitability of spinal motor neurons  
- **Duration:** 2–6 weeks depending on degree of encephalisation  

**21. Mention locomotor effects of spinal shock**  
- Loss of stretch reflexes, atonia and flaccid paralysis, loss of withdrawal and superficial reflexes, muscle atrophy, bone demineralisation and hypercalcaemia, muscle matrix protein degeneration  

**22. Mention autonomic effects of spinal shock**  
- Loss of autonomic reflexes, atony of bladder and rectal walls and sphincters, loss of vasomotor tone causing hypotension  

**23. Describe vascular effects of spinal shock**  
- Decreased arterial blood flow and venous return, cyanosis and poor nutrition of paralysed limbs, bed sores in dependent areas  

**24. Mention causes of reflex recovery after spinal shock**  
- Denervation hypersensitivity, increased collateral sprouting, formation of new excitatory synapses on interneurons and motor neurons  

**25. Explain mass reflex**  
- A massive spinal response due to denervation hypersensitivity, loss of inhibitory control, increased collateral sprouting, irradiation, and reverberating circuits activating large spinal segments simultaneously.  

**26. Mention acute‑stage manifestations of thalamic syndrome**  
- Contralateral loss of all sensations (face and body), secondary hyperalgesia, sensory ataxia  

**27. Mention delayed‑stage manifestations of thalamic syndrome**  
- Partial recovery of crude sensations, permanent loss of fine sensations, hyperalgesia and hyperpathia, emotional disturbances, motor ataxia, paroxysmal pain attacks  

**28. Compare sensory and motor ataxia**  
- **Sensory ataxia:** due to loss of proprioception; finger‑to‑nose test requires visual guidance; positive Romberg sign; stamping gait  
- **Motor ataxia:** due to cerebellar lesion; hypermetria even with eyes open; negative Romberg sign; zigzag gait  

**29. Describe the distribution of the anterior spinal artery**  
- Supplies the medial medulla and anterior two‑thirds of the spinal cord, including anterior and lateral columns, anterior and lateral horns, and base of the dorsal horn.  

**30. Describe the origin of posterior spinal arteries**  
- Arise from the vertebral artery or more commonly from its posterior inferior cerebellar branch.  

**31. Describe the distribution of posterior spinal arteries**  
- Supply the posterior one‑third of the spinal cord, posterior white columns, and posterior horns.  

**32. Describe the origin of radicular arteries**  
- Arise from vertebral, ascending cervical, posterior intercostal, and first lumbar arteries.  

**33. Mention an infectious cause of LMN lesion**  
- Poliomyelitis  

**34. Describe the effects of UMN lesion on reflex activity**  
- Hypertonia due to loss of supraspinal inhibition; spasticity of clasp‑knife type; hyperreflexia and clonus; loss of superficial reflexes; positive Babinski sign  

**35. Describe the effects of UMN lesion on voluntary movements**  
- Contralateral hemiplegia (ipsilateral if lesion below medulla); recovery of gross movements after ~2 weeks; persistent loss of fine distal movements; patient walks with circumduction gait  

**36. Define fasciculation and fibrillation with causes**  
- **Fasciculation:** visible, jerky contraction of muscle fibre groups; due to pathological discharge of spinal motor neurons  
- **Fibrillation:** invisible contraction of individual muscle fibres; detected electrophysiologically; due to denervation hypersensitivity to acetylcholine  

**37. Describe the effects of LMN lesion**  
- Ipsilateral flaccid paralysis, loss of muscle tone and reflexes, marked muscle atrophy due to loss of trophic support, fasciculations and fibrillations

## Cerebellum

**1. Mention differences between cerebrum and cerebellum**  
- Cerebellum is not involved in sensory perception  
- Cerebellar lesion does not cause paralysis  
- Cerebellar body representation is ipsilateral  
- Body representation: vermis and intermediate cerebellar hemisphere; two maps: upper map (anterior lobe): ipsilateral and inverted; lower map (posterior lobe): ipsilateral and upright; audio‑visual representation is bilateral and upright  

**2. Describe the output of the spinocerebellum**  
- From vermis → fastigial nucleus → brainstem  
- From intermediate zone → interposed nuclei → VA & VL nuclei of thalamus → cerebral cortex; midline thalamic structures → basal ganglia, reticular formation, and red nucleus  

**3. Describe the output of the cerebrocerebellum**  
- Cerebrocerebellar cortex → dentate nucleus → VA & VL nuclei of thalamus → cerebral cortex  

**4. Mossy fibre circuit is concerned with ______, and its effect on Purkinje cells results in ______**  
- Well‑learned movements  
- Single‑spike action potentials  

**5. Describe the effect of climbing fibres on Purkinje cells**  
- Act directly on Purkinje cells to modify their sensitivity  
- In overshooting: increase Purkinje sensitivity → rapid inhibition of deep cerebellar nuclei → correction of overshoot  
- In undershooting: decrease Purkinje sensitivity → delayed inhibition → correction of undershoot  
- Produce conditioning of Purkinje cells  
- Cause complex, multi‑peaked action potentials  

**6. Mention functions of vestibulocerebellum**  
- Maintenance of equilibrium and body posture  
- Control of eye movements (vestibulo‑ocular reflex)  

**7. Mention functions of spinocerebellum**  
- Servo‑comparator function  
- Control of ballistic movements (via mossy fibres)  
- Damping function  

**8. Mention the inputs of the spinocerebellum for servo‑comparator function**  
- Information from motor cortex and red nucleus about intended movement plan  
- Sensory feedback from body about actual movement  
- Efference copy of motor signals reaching anterior horn cells

## Functions Of Reticular Formation

**1. PRF is located in**  
- Midbrain & pons  

**2. Describe functions of ascending & descending fibres of PRF**  
- **Ascending fibres** go first to thalamus (intralaminar & reticular nuclei) which, in turn, project diffusely & non‑specifically to almost all parts of cerebral cortex → responsible for arousal.  
- **Descending fibres** increase antigravity muscle tone.  

**3. The activity of reticular excitatory area is inhibited by**  
- Reticular inhibitory area, inhibitory areas of cerebral cortex, paleocerebellum, basal ganglia (the same activate inhibitory reticular formation).  

**4. Functions of medullary reticular nuclei**  
- Decrease brain activity (inhibit PRF)  
- Decrease antigravity muscle tone  

**5. Mention factors stimulating RAS**  
- Sensory signals from periphery especially pain  
- Signals from cerebral cortex  
- Analeptics & sympathomimetics  
- Stress  
- Caffeine  
- Thyroid hormone  

**6. Mention factors inhibiting RAS**  
- Lack of sensory input signals  
- Signals from sleep centres  
- General anaesthesia  
- Damage by tumour or vascular lesion  

**7. Enumerate functions of reticular formation**  
- Vital link between higher centres in brain & lower centres in spinal cord  
- Maintain posture & equilibrium and muscle tone (continuous facilitatory impulses from reticular formation & vestibular nuclei)  
- Arousal & consciousness  
- RF contains vital centres for heart rate, blood pressure, respiration & many digestive activities  
- Pain modulation (raphe magnus nucleus)  
- REM sleep  

**8. Compare primary & secondary evoked potentials**  
- **Primary:** +ve wave followed by –ve wave, recorded from specific localised brain area.  
- **Secondary:** follow primary potentials, large prolonged +ve wave, non‑localised, due to activation of cortical neurons by impulses from non‑specific thalamic nuclei.  

**9. Mention uses of evoked potentials**  
- Mapping of brain areas  
- Diagnosis of diseases  

**10. EEG is a measure of**  
- Summation of polysynaptic potentials of neurons in cerebral cortex.  

**11. Describe conditions of performing EEG**  
- Calm room with comfortable temperature  
- Person should be in complete physical & mental rest  

**12. Compare alpha and beta waves**

**13. Compare theta and delta waves**  
- **Theta:** children & adults during light sleep and emotional stress; temporal & parietal regions; amplitude 100 µV; frequency 4–7 cycles/sec  
- **Delta:** infants during deep sleep, brain damage & surgical anaesthesia; all cortical areas; amplitude 100 µV; frequency ~3.5 cycles/sec  

**14. Describe gamma waves**  
- Wave of hyper‑arousal and higher mental activity; recorded from all cortical areas; amplitude 0.5–2 µV; frequency 30–80 Hz  

**15. Value of EEG**  
- Non‑invasive test for brain function  
- Localisation of tumours  
- Monitoring sleep stages & diagnosing sleep disorders  
- Diagnosis of epilepsy  
- Indicates brain death  

**16. Give reason for occurrence of alpha block**  
- Stimulation of ascending reticular activating system  

**17. Mention functions of sleep**

## Memory, Learning & Amnesia

**1. Mention areas concerned with declarative memory**  
- Hippocampus, medial temporal lobes of the brain, neocortex & prefrontal cortex  

**2. Mention types of explicit memory**  
- Semantic memory: general memories, facts  
- Episodic memory: personal memories (events & experiences)  

**3. Mention types of implicit memories & areas responsible**  
- Priming: facilitation of recognition of words/objects — neocortex  
- Procedural memory: skills & habits which become automatic & unconscious — striatum & cerebellum  
- Associative learning: classical & operant conditioning — amygdala & cerebellum  
- Non‑associative learning: habituation & sensitisation — various reflex pathways  

**4. Describe mechanism of storage of short‑ & long‑term memories**  
- **Short‑term memory:** involves transient modifications in functions of pre‑existing synapses, such as alteration in amount of neurotransmitter released or temporary increase in responsiveness of the postsynaptic cell to neurotransmitter.  
- **Long‑term memory:** involves relatively permanent functional or structural changes at synapses and new protein formation, such as increased vesicle release sites, increased transmitter vesicles, increased presynaptic terminals, and changes in structure of dendritic spines permitting transmission of stronger signals.  

**5. Compare site of storage of short‑ & long‑term memory**  
- Short‑term memory: stored in frontal lobe; explicit memory encoded in hippocampus  
- Long‑term memory: stored in various parts of neocortex  

**6. Define working memory**  
- Form of short‑term memory that keeps information available usually for very short periods while the individual plans actions based on it. Prefrontal cortex is its site of operation.  

**7. Define post‑tetanic stimulation and its mechanism**  
- Increase in the postsynaptic response following stimulation of presynaptic neuron by a brief tetanising train of stimuli.  
- Mechanism: accumulation of Ca²⁺ in presynaptic neuron due to tetanising stimulus resulting in increased release of neurotransmitter.  

**8. Define habituation and its mechanism**  
- Decreased responsiveness to repetitive presentation of a neutral stimulus.  
- Mechanism: gradual inactivation of Ca²⁺ channels in presynaptic neurons with repeated stimulation, leading to decreased intracellular Ca²⁺ and decreased neurotransmitter release.  

**9. Define sensitization**  
- Prolonged occurrence of augmented postsynaptic response to a stimulus to which habituation had previously occurred, due to association with a noxious stimulus.  

**10. Describe mechanism of sensitisation**  
- Noxious stimulus causes serotonin release from facilitatory interneuron onto presynaptic terminal.  
- Serotonin receptor activation on sensory terminal membrane stimulates adenyl cyclase → increased cAMP formation → K⁺ channel blockade → prolonged action potential in synaptic terminals → increased Ca²⁺ influx → increased neurotransmitter release.  

**11. Describe mechanism of LTP**  
- Long‑term potentiation occurs through multiple mechanisms; some depend on changes in NMDA receptors while others are NMDA‑independent. It is initiated by increased intracellular Ca²⁺ in either presynaptic or postsynaptic neuron.  

**12. Describe mechanism & value of LTD**  
- **Mechanism:** produced by slower stimulation of presynaptic neurons and associated with a smaller rise in intracellular Ca²⁺ within postsynaptic neuron compared to LTP.  
- **Value:** mechanism of learning in cerebellum.  

**13. Compare causes of anterograde & retrograde amnesia**  
- Anterograde amnesia: atherosclerosis, old age, lesions of hippocampus or medial temporal lobe  
- Retrograde amnesia: brain concussion, anaesthesia, electroshock therapy  

**14. Neurotransmitter affected in Alzheimer’s disease**  
- Acetylcholine  

**15. Compare classical & operant conditioning**  
- **Classical conditioning:** conditioned reflex is a reflex response to a neutral stimulus; involves repetitive pairing of conditioned stimulus with unconditioned stimulus.  
- **Operant conditioning:** modification of behaviour using consequences; involves reinforcement or punishment after behaviour.

**10. Compare motor and sensory cortices**  
- **Motor:** thickest zone, small and medium pyramidal cells in layer III, large in layer V; granule cells rare or absent  
- **Sensory:** relatively thinner than motor cortex; contains closely packed granule cells; pyramidal cells ill‑defined  

**11. The dura is formed of ….., inner surface is lined by …..**  
- Dense fibro‑elastic connective tissue  
- Simple squamous epithelium  

**12. The arachnoid is formed of ….. & …..**  
- Flat membrane adjacent to dura  
- System of trabeculae composed of collagen fibres and fibroblasts  

**13. Function of arachnoid villi**  
- Help in CSF circulation from subarachnoid space into blood sinuses of dura  

**14. Pial cells are joined by ….., and separated from brain tissue by …..**  
- Desmosomes, gap junctions, and few tight junctions  
- Astrocytic processes  

**15. Describe composition of choroid plexus villi, and function**  
- **Composition:** central core of pia mater with fenestrated blood capillaries, covered by columnar ependymal cells which line brain ventricles  
- **Function:** secretion of CSF  

**16. Mention causes of hydrocephalus**  
- Decreased absorption of CSF  
- Obstruction of CSF outflow from ventricles  

**17. Neuroglia can be detected using …..**  
- Silver or gold impregnation methods  

**18. Types of oligodendrocytes and their function**  
- **Light cells:** predominant in CNS of fetus & neonate; large euchromatic nuclei, pale cytoplasm; capable of division  
- **Dark cells:** predominant in CNS of adults; heterochromatic nuclei, dense cytoplasm  
- **Function:** form myelin sheath in CNS (each cell can myelinate several internodal segments on same or different axons), support neurons, insulate nerve impulses  

**19. Function of the pool of adult progenitor cells**  
- Differentiate into oligodendrocytes and remyelinate pathologically demyelinated regions  

**20. Astrocyte feet are in contact with ….., connected by …..**  
- Blood capillaries, neurons, or nerve fibres  
- Gap junctions  

**21. Mention contents of cytoplasm of astrocytes**  
- Microtubules  
- Intermediate filaments (glial filaments) — formed of GFAP, used as marker for brain tumours  
- Thread‑like mitochondria  

**22. Describe types of astrocytes**  
- **Fibrous astrocytes:** white matter; long, thin processes  
- **Protoplasmic astrocytes:** grey matter; short, extensive processes  
- Müller cells in retina, pituicytes in posterior pituitary  

**23. Mention functions of astrocytes**  
- Regulate metabolic exchange between neurons and blood  
- Secrete factors regulating neural functions  
- Regulate extracellular ionic concentration around neurons  
- Regulate diameter of blood vessels within CNS  
- Support neurons  
- Form part of BBB  
- Repair processes via proliferation and formation of glial tissue (glial scar)  

**24. Mention functions of microglia**  
- Phagocytose CNS debris  
- Have receptors for neurotransmitters — function in synaptic transmission  
- Phagocytose apoptotic cells during development  

**25. Describe site & shape of ependymal cells**  
- **Site:** line ventricles of brain and central canal of spinal cord  
- **Shape:** squamous, cuboidal, or columnar; do not rest on basement membrane; apical surface has numerous microvilli and/or cilia  

**26. Describe the ependymal cells lining 3rd and 4th ventricles**  
- Numerous microvilli, rarely cilia  
- Flattened basal nuclei  
- Numerous mitochondria  
- Joined by tight junctions and desmosomes  

**27. Mention functions of tanycytes**  
- Basal processes facilitate passage of hypothalamic‑regulating hormones to pars distalis of pituitary gland  

**28. Enumerate functions of ependyma**  
- Circulation of CSF via cilia  
- Absorptive function via microvilli  
- Some ependymal cells may act as stem cells in adult brain (neural regeneration potential under investigation)  

**29. Mention components of blood‑brain barrier (BBB)**  
- Endothelial cells of continuous capillaries with tight junctions  
- Complete basement membrane of capillaries  
- Pericytes with perivascular microglia  
- Layer of perivascular astrocytic feet  

**30. Mention areas where BBB is lacking**  
- Endocrine regions: posterior pituitary and pineal body  
- Hypothalamus — monitors plasma contents  
- Choroid plexus — CSF production site  

**31. Function of BBB**  
- Protects brain tissue against toxic drugs and harmful substances, e.g., bacteria in blood  

**32. (blank)**  

**33. Mention components of blood‑CSF barrier**  
- Endothelial cells of fenestrated blood capillaries  
- Basement membrane of endothelial cells  
- Connective tissue on which ependymal cells rest  
- Ependymal cells joined by apical junctional complex  

**34. Mention components of brain‑CSF barrier**  
- Ependymal cells connected by apical junctional complex  
- Connective tissue on which ependymal cells rest  
- Foot processes of astrocytes

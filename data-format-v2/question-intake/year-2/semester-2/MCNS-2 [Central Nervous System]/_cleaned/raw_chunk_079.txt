AN1. [***] A tumor in the floor of the fourth ventricle (facial colliculus region) most directly damages which nucleus?

PH1. [***] A patient with an upper motor neuron lesion develops spastic hypertonia. This is mainly due to:

B. Dopaminergic

A. Releasing histamine locally

E. A cranial ganglion
Answer: D — The optic nerve is a CNS tract, not a true peripheral nerve.

HI38. [*] Following CNS injury, the glial scar is formed mainly by:
A. Microglia
B. Oligodendrocytes
C. Astrocytes
D. Ependymal cells
E. Schwann cells
Answer: C — Reactive astrocytes (astrogliosis) form the scar.

PM1. [**] Levodopa rather than dopamine is used in Parkinson disease because levodopa:
A. Is more potent
B. Has no side effects
C. Acts directly on serotonin receptors
D. Crosses the blood–brain barrier
E. Is an enzyme inhibitor
Answer: D — Dopamine cannot cross the BBB, but its precursor L-DOPA can.

PM2. [**] Carbidopa is combined with levodopa because it:
A. Crosses into the brain
B. Blocks central dopamine receptors directly
C. Is itself converted to dopamine
D. Stimulates the vomiting center
E. Inhibits peripheral DOPA decarboxylase
Answer: E — It reduces peripheral conversion, increasing brain delivery.

PM3. [*] Selegiline aids Parkinson disease by inhibiting:
A. COMT
B. DOPA decarboxylase
C. Tyrosine hydroxylase
D. Monoamine oxidase B
E. Acetylcholinesterase
Answer: D — MAO-B inhibition slows dopamine breakdown.

PM4. [*] Entacapone prolongs levodopa action by inhibiting:
A. MAO-B
B. DOPA decarboxylase
C. Catechol-O-methyltransferase
D. Acetylcholinesterase
E. Dopamine β-hydroxylase enzyme
Answer: C — COMT inhibition reduces levodopa metabolism.

PM5. [*] The Parkinsonian tremor responds to anticholinergic drugs such as:
A. Benztropine
B. Neostigmine
C. Pilocarpine
D. Bethanechol
E. Physostigmine
Answer: A — Central antimuscarinics relieve tremor and rigidity.

PM6. [*] Bromocriptine acts in Parkinson disease as a:
A. Dopamine antagonist
B. MAO inhibitor
C. Cholinergic agonist
D. A voltage-gated sodium-channel blocker
E. Dopamine (D2) receptor agonist
Answer: E — It directly stimulates dopamine receptors.

PM7. [**] Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A function by increasing the:
A. Duration of each chloride channel opening
B. Sodium influx
C. Calcium release
D. Potassium efflux
E. Frequency of chloride channel opening
Answer: E — They raise the frequency of Cl− channel opening.

PM8. [*] Barbiturates potentiate GABA-A by increasing the:
A. Frequency of chloride channel opening
B. Sodium conductance
C. Calcium influx
D. Duration of chloride channel opening
E. Receptor number
Answer: D — They prolong each channel opening.

PM9. [**] Phenytoin controls seizures mainly by blocking:
A. Voltage-gated calcium channels
B. Potassium channels
C. GABA receptors
D. Voltage-gated sodium channels
E. NMDA receptors
Answer: D — It stabilizes inactivated Na+ channels.

PM10. [*] The drug of first choice for trigeminal neuralgia is:
A. Morphine sulfate
B. Carbamazepine
C. Aspirin
D. Diazepam
E. Levodopa
Answer: B — Carbamazepine is first-line for trigeminal neuralgia.

PM11. [*] Ethosuximide controls absence seizures by blocking:
A. Sodium channels
B. GABA transaminase enzyme
C. T-type calcium channels
D. NMDA receptors
E. Potassium channels
Answer: C — It blocks thalamic T-type Ca2+ channels.

PM12. [*] A broad-spectrum antiepileptic effective in many seizure types is:
A. Oral ethosuximide
B. Vigabatrin
C. Bromocriptine
D. Atropine
E. Sodium valproate
Answer: E — Valproate works in generalized and partial seizures.

PM13. [*] The first-line drug for terminating status epilepticus is an intravenous:
A. Continuous intravenous barbiturate infusion
B. Benzodiazepine (e.g., lorazepam)
C. Phenytoin load only
D. Opioid
E. Antipsychotic
Answer: B — IV benzodiazepines abort status epilepticus.

PM14. [*] Local anesthetics such as lidocaine act by blocking:
A. Sodium channels
B. Potassium channels
C. Calcium channels
D. GABA receptors
E. Acetylcholinesterase
Answer: A — They block voltage-gated Na+ channels in axons.

PM15. [**] The analgesic action of morphine is mediated mainly by:
A. GABA-A receptors
B. NMDA receptors
C. Beta-adrenergic receptors
D. Nicotinic receptors
E. Mu opioid receptors
Answer: E — μ-receptor agonism produces analgesia.

PM16. [*] The specific antidote for opioid overdose is:
A. Flumazenil
B. Atropine
C. Naloxone
D. Physostigmine
E. Protamine
Answer: C — Naloxone is a competitive opioid antagonist.

PM17. [*] Fluoxetine relieves depression by:
A. Blocking dopamine D2 receptors
B. Inhibiting monoamine oxidase
C. Blocking sodium channels
D. Inhibiting serotonin reuptake
E. Releasing acetylcholine
Answer: D — SSRIs block presynaptic serotonin reuptake.

PM18. [*] Tricyclic antidepressants act mainly by:
A. Inhibiting MAO
B. Selectively blocking dopamine D2 receptors in the brain
C. Opening chloride channels
D. Inhibiting noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake
E. Blocking calcium channels
Answer: D — They inhibit reuptake of NE and 5-HT.

PM19. [*] The drug of choice for long-term control of bipolar disorder is:
A. Lithium
B. Levodopa
C. Phenytoin
D. Atropine
E. Morphine
Answer: A — Lithium is a mood stabilizer for bipolar disorder.

PM20. [**] Typical antipsychotics produce extrapyramidal side effects because they block:
A. Dopamine D2 receptors
B. Muscarinic cholinergic receptors
C. Serotonin receptors
D. GABA receptors
E. Histamine receptors
Answer: A — Nigrostriatal D2 blockade causes Parkinsonism/dystonia.

PM21. [*] Long-term haloperidol use may cause involuntary orofacial movements known as:
A. A pill-rolling resting tremor
B. Chorea minor
C. Myoclonus
D. Hemiballismus
E. Tardive dyskinesia
Answer: E — Tardive dyskinesia follows chronic D2 blockade.

PM22. [*] The atypical antipsychotic that requires white-cell monitoring (agranulocytosis) is:
A. Haloperidol
B. Chlorpromazine
C. Fluphenazine
D. Sulpiride
E. Clozapine
Answer: E — Clozapine can cause agranulocytosis.

PM23. [*] Symptomatic treatment of Alzheimer disease uses inhibitors of:
A. Monoamine oxidase type B at the synapse
B. COMT
C. Acetylcholinesterase (e.g., donepezil)
D. Sodium channels
E. Dopamine reuptake
Answer: C — Cholinesterase inhibitors raise synaptic ACh.

PM24. [*] Memantine, used in Alzheimer disease, is a:
A. NMDA receptor antagonist
B. Cholinesterase inhibitor
C. Dopamine agonist
D. GABA agonist
E. Sodium-channel blocker
Answer: A — It blocks excitotoxic NMDA-receptor activity.

PM25. [*] Sumatriptan aborts migraine by acting as a:
A. Dopamine antagonist
B. Beta-receptor agonist
C. Calcium-channel blocker
D. Histamine agonist
E. 5-HT1 receptor agonist
Answer: E — 5-HT1B/1D agonism causes cranial vasoconstriction.

PM26. [*] Essential (familial) tremor is best treated with:
A. Oral levodopa
B. Atropine
C. Morphine
D. Propranolol
E. Phenytoin
Answer: D — The beta-blocker propranolol reduces essential tremor.

PM27. [*] Baclofen relieves spasticity by acting as an agonist at:
A. GABA-A receptors
B. NMDA receptors
C. Nicotinic receptors
D. GABA-B receptors
E. Opioid receptors
Answer: D — GABA-B agonism reduces spinal excitatory transmission.

PM28. [*] Botulinum toxin produces flaccid paralysis by:
A. Competitively blocking postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors
B. Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase
C. Blocking acetylcholine release at the nerve terminal
D. Depleting dopamine
E. Blocking sodium channels
Answer: C — It prevents ACh exocytosis at the neuromuscular junction.

PM29. [*] Atropine differs from glycopyrrolate in that it:
A. Acts instead as a direct muscarinic receptor agonist
B. Crosses the blood–brain barrier (central effects)
C. Blocks nicotinic receptors
D. Stimulates sweating
E. Causes bradycardia
Answer: B — As a tertiary amine it has central antimuscarinic effects.

PM30. [*] Strychnine causes convulsions by antagonizing:
A. GABA receptors
B. Dopamine receptors
C. Nicotinic receptors
D. NMDA receptors
E. Glycine receptors
Answer: E — Loss of glycine inhibition produces tonic spasms.

PM31. [*] Tetanus toxin produces spastic paralysis by blocking release of:
A. Acetylcholine only
B. Glycine and GABA
C. Dopamine
D. Noradrenaline
E. Histamine
Answer: B — It prevents inhibitory transmitter release from Renshaw cells.

PM32. [*] Neostigmine improves myasthenia gravis by:
A. Irreversibly blocking acetylcholine receptors
B. Releasing dopamine
C. Blocking sodium channels
D. Reversibly inhibiting acetylcholinesterase
E. Stimulating GABA receptors
Answer: D — It raises ACh at the neuromuscular junction.

PM33. [*] Amphetamine produces central stimulation chiefly by:
A. Releasing catecholamines from terminals
B. Blocking presynaptic dopamine receptors
C. Inhibiting MAO only
D. Activating GABA receptors
E. Blocking sodium channels
Answer: A — It promotes release of dopamine and noradrenaline.

PM34. [*] Cocaine produces its central effects mainly by:
A. Releasing acetylcholine
B. Directly activating opioid receptors
C. Inhibiting COMT
D. Opening chloride channels
E. Blocking catecholamine reuptake
Answer: E — It blocks reuptake of dopamine/noradrenaline.

PM35. [*] The CNS-stimulant action of caffeine is due to antagonism of:
A. Dopamine receptors
B. GABA receptors
C. Histamine receptors
D. Opioid receptors
E. Adenosine receptors
Answer: E — Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors.

PM36. [*] The only drug shown to prolong survival in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is:
A. Riluzole
B. Levodopa
C. Donepezil
D. Baclofen
E. Phenytoin
Answer: A — Riluzole reduces glutamate excitotoxicity.

PM37. [*] Chorea of Huntington disease can be reduced by tetrabenazine, which:
A. Blocks postsynaptic dopamine receptors directly
B. Stimulates dopamine release
C. Inhibits acetylcholinesterase
D. Depletes dopamine by inhibiting VMAT2

Answer: D — VMAT2 inhibition depletes presynaptic dopamine.

A. The α2δ subunit of calcium channels

D. NMDA receptors
E. GABA-A receptors

PM39. [*] Dantrolene treats malignant hyperthermia by acting on the:
A. The presynaptic neuromuscular junction terminal

C. GABA receptor
D. Motor cortex

PM40. [*] A characteristic chronic adverse effect of phenytoin is:

E. Renal stones

PM41. [*] The drug commonly given rectally to abort a prolonged febrile seizure in a child is:

PM42. [*] Vigabatrin raises brain GABA by inhibiting:
A. Glutamate decarboxylase

PM43. [*] After several years of levodopa therapy, a common problem is:

PM44. [*] Amantadine is useful in Parkinson disease because it:
A. Blocks dopamine receptors

PM45. [*] Excessive daytime sleepiness of narcolepsy is treated with:
A. Diazepam

PM46. [*] The stimulant used to treat attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder is:
A. Diazepam tablets

PM47. [*] A patient on a monoamine oxidase inhibitor who eats aged cheese may develop:

PM48. [*] High fever, rigidity, and autonomic instability after antipsychotics suggest:

PM49. [*] Ergotamine relieves migraine mainly through:

PM51. [*] Physostigmine differs from neostigmine in that it:

PM54. [*] Flumazenil is used to reverse the effects of:

E. Demyelination of white matter

A. Glioblastoma multiforme

C. Schwannoma

A. Amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles
B. Lewy bodies
C. Negri bodies

PA10. [*] Marked atrophy of the caudate nucleus is typical of:
A. Parkinson disease
B. Alzheimer disease
C. Multiple sclerosis
D. Wernicke encephalopathy
E. Huntington disease
Answer: E — Striatal (caudate) degeneration; CAG-repeat disorder.

PA11. [*] Hemorrhagic lesions of the mammillary bodies are characteristic of:
A. Wernicke encephalopathy
B. Huntington disease (chorea)
C. Multiple sclerosis
D. Tabes dorsalis
E. Rabies

PA12. [*] Degeneration of the dorsal columns in neurosyphilis is called:
A. Tabes dorsalis
B. Syringomyelia

PA13. [*] Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis involves degeneration of the:
A. The dorsal columns and spinocerebellar tracts only

E. Anterior horn cells and corticospinal tracts

PA14. [*] A reliable sign of chronically raised intracranial pressure is:
A. Miosis

PA15. [*] The earliest microscopic marker of irreversible neuronal ischemia is the:
A. Lewy body
B. Negri body
C. Neurofibrillary tangle formation
D. Psammoma body

PA16. [*] Hydrocephalus due to obstruction within the ventricular system is termed:

PA18. [*] Neurofibrillary tangles are composed of:

SECTION 10 — ESSAY QUESTIONS (with model answers)
========================================

ES1. [**] Describe the internal capsule: its parts, the main fibers in each, its blood supply, and the effects of a lesion.
Model answer:
- A V-shaped band of white matter between the caudate/thalamus (medially) and the lentiform nucleus (laterally).
- Parts: anterior limb, genu, posterior limb, retrolentiform and sublentiform parts.
- Anterior limb: frontopontine + anterior thalamic radiation. Genu: corticonuclear (corticobulbar) fibers. Posterior limb: corticospinal, sensory (thalamocortical) fibers, with optic and auditory radiations in the retro-/sublentiform parts.
- Blood supply: chiefly the lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral artery (genu also by branches of the internal carotid/anterior choroidal).
- Lesion (e.g., capsular stroke): contralateral spastic hemiplegia, hemisensory loss, and homonymous hemianopia ("three-hemi" syndrome).

ES2. [**] Describe the circle of Willis: its components, functional importance, and one clinical correlation.
Model answer:
- An arterial anastomotic ring at the base of the brain in the interpeduncular fossa.
- Formed by: anterior communicating artery, two anterior cerebral arteries, two internal carotids, two posterior communicating arteries, and two posterior cerebral arteries (from the basilar).
- Function: equalizes blood flow and provides collateral circulation if one vessel is occluded.
- Clinical: the commonest site of berry aneurysms (especially the anterior communicating artery), which rupture into the subarachnoid space.

ES3. [**] Describe the cerebellum: functional divisions, main connections, and the signs of a cerebellar lesion.
Model answer:
- Functional divisions: vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular – equilibrium), spinocerebellum (vermis/paravermis – muscle tone and posture), and cerebrocerebellum (lateral hemispheres – planning/coordination of skilled movement).
- Connections via three peduncles: inferior (input from cord and medulla), middle (pontocerebellar input from cortex), superior (output to red nucleus/thalamus).
- Lesion signs (ipsilateral): ataxia, intention tremor, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, nystagmus, scanning/dysarthric speech, and hypotonia.

ES4. [**] Trace the visual pathway and give the field defect produced by a lesion at each level.
Model answer:
- Retina → optic nerve → optic chiasma → optic tract → lateral geniculate body → optic radiation → primary visual cortex (area 17, calcarine sulcus).
- Optic nerve lesion: blindness of that eye. Chiasma (midline) lesion: bitemporal hemianopia. Optic tract lesion: contralateral homonymous hemianopia.
- Temporal (Meyer's loop) radiation: contralateral upper quadrantanopia; parietal radiation: lower quadrantanopia.
- Occipital cortex lesion: contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.

ES5. [**] Describe the pyramidal (corticospinal) tract: origin, course, decussation, and the effects of an upper motor neuron lesion.
Model answer:
- Origin: pyramidal cells of the motor and premotor cortex (including Betz cells, layer V).
- Course: corona radiata → posterior limb of internal capsule → crus cerebri → pons → medullary pyramids.
- ~85% decussate in the lower medulla to form the lateral corticospinal tract; the rest form the anterior corticospinal tract (decussating segmentally).
- UMN lesion: contralateral spastic paralysis, hypertonia, hyperreflexia, clonus, lost superficial (abdominal) reflexes, and an extensor plantar (Babinski) response.

ES6. [**] Describe the cavernous sinus: its contents and relations, and the consequences of thrombosis.
Model answer:
- A paired dural venous sinus on each side of the body of the sphenoid/sella turcica.
- Within it: the internal carotid artery and the abducent nerve (VI).
- In its lateral wall (top to bottom): oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV), ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) nerves. (V3 does NOT pass through it.)
- Communications: ophthalmic veins, pterygoid plexus (via emissary veins), and the petrosal sinuses.
- Thrombosis: painful ophthalmoplegia, proptosis, chemosis, and sensory loss over V1–V2.

ES7. [**] Describe the formation, circulation, absorption, and functions of cerebrospinal fluid.
Model answer:
- Formation: ~500 mL/day by the choroid plexuses of the lateral, third and fourth ventricles (total volume ~150 mL).
- Circulation: lateral ventricles → interventricular foramina → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → apertures of Luschka and Magendie → subarachnoid space.
- Absorption: through the arachnoid villi/granulations into the superior sagittal (dural venous) sinus.
- Functions: buoyancy and protection (cushioning), maintenance of a stable chemical environment, and removal of metabolites.
- Obstruction causes hydrocephalus (non-communicating if within the ventricular system).

ES8. [*] Outline the functions of the hypothalamus.
Model answer:
- Autonomic control: anterior/preoptic = parasympathetic and heat loss; posterior/lateral = sympathetic and heat conservation.
- Endocrine control via releasing/inhibiting hormones to the anterior pituitary, and ADH/oxytocin to the posterior pituitary.
- Temperature regulation, food intake (lateral = hunger; ventromedial = satiety), thirst and water balance.
- Circadian rhythm (suprachiasmatic nucleus) and emotional/behavioral responses (with the limbic system).

ES9. [**] Describe the basal ganglia: components, the direct and indirect pathways, and related movement disorders.
Model answer:
- Components: caudate + putamen (striatum), globus pallidus (interna/externa), subthalamic nucleus, and substantia nigra.
- Direct pathway (facilitates movement): cortex → striatum → GPi/SNr → thalamus → cortex; dopamine (D1) enhances it.
- Indirect pathway (suppresses movement): striatum → GPe → subthalamic nucleus → GPi; dopamine (D2) inhibits it.
- Disorders: Parkinson disease (nigral dopamine loss – rigidity, bradykinesia, resting tremor); Huntington chorea (striatal loss); hemiballismus (subthalamic lesion).

ES10. [*] Describe the thalamus: its major nuclei and connections, and the thalamic syndrome.
Model answer:
- A large grey-matter relay between subcortical structures and the cortex.
- Specific relay nuclei: VPL (body sensation), VPM (face), LGB (vision), MGB (hearing), VA/VL (motor, from basal ganglia/cerebellum), anterior nucleus (limbic, from mammillary body).
- Association nuclei (dorsomedial, pulvinar) and non-specific intralaminar nuclei (arousal).
- Thalamic syndrome (Dejerine–Roussy): contralateral sensory loss followed by spontaneous, poorly localized pain.

ES11. [**] Describe the arterial blood supply of the brain and give two common stroke syndromes.
Model answer:
- Two systems: internal carotid (anterior circulation) and vertebrobasilar (posterior), joined by the circle of Willis.
- Anterior cerebral artery: medial frontal/parietal cortex (leg area). Middle cerebral artery: lateral hemisphere (face/arm, language). Posterior cerebral artery: occipital lobe and inferomedial temporal lobe.
- MCA stroke: contralateral face/arm weakness and sensory loss, with aphasia if dominant hemisphere.
- ACA stroke: contralateral leg weakness. PCA stroke: contralateral homonymous hemianopia.

ES12. [*] List the main ascending and descending tracts of the spinal cord and their functions.
Model answer:
- Ascending: dorsal columns (gracile/cuneate) – fine touch, vibration, conscious proprioception; lateral spinothalamic – pain and temperature; anterior spinothalamic – crude touch; spinocerebellar – unconscious proprioception.
- Descending: lateral and anterior corticospinal – voluntary movement; rubrospinal, vestibulospinal, reticulospinal and tectospinal – posture, tone and reflex movement.
- Dorsal columns and corticospinal tract are clinically the most important for localization.

ES13. [**] Describe the facial nerve: its functional components and the difference between an upper and a lower motor neuron facial palsy.
Model answer:
- Components: motor to muscles of facial expression; special sensory (taste, anterior two-thirds of tongue, via chorda tympani); parasympathetic to lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands.
- Course: emerges at the cerebellopontine angle → internal acoustic meatus → facial canal (geniculate ganglion, gives chorda tympani and nerve to stapedius) → stylomastoid foramen → parotid plexus.
- UMN lesion (e.g., stroke): contralateral weakness of the LOWER face only (forehead spared, due to bilateral cortical supply of the upper face).
- LMN lesion (Bell palsy): weakness of the WHOLE half of the face, with loss of forehead wrinkling and inability to close the eye.

ES14. [*] Describe the trigeminal nerve: its divisions, functions, and the features of a lesion.
Model answer:
- The main sensory nerve of the face and motor nerve of mastication.
- Three divisions: ophthalmic (V1, sensory), maxillary (V2, sensory), mandibular (V3, sensory + motor).
- Sensory: face, scalp, cornea, mucosa of mouth/nose, anterior two-thirds of tongue (general sensation). Motor: muscles of mastication.
- Lesion: ipsilateral facial sensory loss, loss of corneal reflex, and deviation of the jaw to the affected side; irritation causes trigeminal neuralgia.

ES15. [*] Describe the limbic system: its components and functions.
Model answer:
- Components: hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, mammillary bodies, fornix, and parts of the hypothalamus and thalamus (anterior nucleus).
- The Papez circuit: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary body → anterior thalamic nucleus → cingulate gyrus → back to hippocampus.
- Functions: emotion (amygdala – fear/aggression), memory formation (hippocampus), motivation, and autonomic/endocrine responses to emotion.

ES16. [*] Describe the reticular formation and the ascending reticular activating system.
Model answer:
- A diffuse network of neurons in the core of the brainstem (medulla, pons, midbrain).
- Functions: control of consciousness and the sleep–wake cycle (ARAS), modulation of muscle tone and posture, cardiovascular and respiratory control, and pain modulation.
- The ascending reticular activating system projects to the cortex (via the thalamus) to maintain wakefulness; its damage causes coma.

ES17. [*] Describe the meninges and the dural venous sinuses.
Model answer:
- Three layers: dura mater (tough, two layers – periosteal and meningeal), arachnoid mater (avascular, deep to dura), and pia mater (vascular, adherent to brain).
- Dural folds: falx cerebri, tentorium cerebelli, falx cerebelli, diaphragma sellae.
- Spaces: extradural (potential), subdural (potential), subarachnoid (contains CSF and vessels).
- Venous sinuses (between dural layers): superior and inferior sagittal, straight, transverse, sigmoid, cavernous – draining ultimately to the internal jugular vein.

ES18. [*] Describe the ventricular system of the brain.
Model answer:
- Two lateral ventricles (in the cerebral hemispheres) each communicating through an interventricular foramen (of Monro) with the third ventricle.
- The third ventricle (in the diencephalon) connects via the cerebral aqueduct (of Sylvius) to the fourth ventricle.
- The fourth ventricle (between pons/medulla and cerebellum) drains through the median aperture (Magendie) and two lateral apertures (Luschka) into the subarachnoid space.
- All are lined by ependyma and contain choroid plexus that secretes CSF.

ES19. [*] Compare the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the autonomic nervous system.
Model answer:
- Sympathetic (thoracolumbar, T1–L2): short preganglionic, long postganglionic fibers; paravertebral/prevertebral ganglia; "fight or flight" – raises heart rate, dilates pupils/bronchi, inhibits gut.
- Parasympathetic (craniosacral; CN III, VII, IX, X and S2–S4): long preganglionic, short postganglionic fibers; ganglia near the organ; "rest and digest" – slows heart, constricts pupils, stimulates gut.
- Transmitters: all preganglionic and parasympathetic postganglionic are cholinergic; most sympathetic postganglionic are noradrenergic (sweat glands cholinergic).

ES20. [*] Outline the sequence of events in chemical synaptic transmission.
Model answer:
- An action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal and opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels.
- Ca2+ influx triggers exocytosis of neurotransmitter from synaptic vesicles into the cleft.
- Transmitter diffuses across and binds postsynaptic receptors, producing an EPSP (e.g., glutamate) or IPSP (e.g., GABA/glycine).
- Action is terminated by reuptake, enzymatic degradation (e.g., acetylcholinesterase), or diffusion.

ES21. [*] Describe the pathway for pain and its modulation.
Model answer:
- First-order neurons (A-delta and C fibers) enter the dorsal horn and synapse (substance P, glutamate).
- Second-order neurons cross and ascend in the lateral spinothalamic tract to the VPL of the thalamus; third-order neurons project to the sensory cortex.
- Gate-control: large A-beta touch fibers inhibit pain transmission in the dorsal horn.
- Descending modulation: periaqueductal grey → raphe nuclei → dorsal horn, using endogenous opioids (enkephalins), serotonin and noradrenaline.

ES22. [*] Describe the stages of sleep and their EEG features.
Model answer:
- Awake: beta waves (alert) and alpha waves (relaxed, eyes closed).
- Non-REM sleep: stage 1 (theta), stage 2 (sleep spindles and K-complexes), stages 3–4 (delta, deep slow-wave sleep).
- REM (paradoxical) sleep: low-voltage fast EEG resembling wakefulness, rapid eye movements, muscle atonia, and vivid dreaming.
- Cycles of ~90 minutes; REM proportion increases towards morning.

ES23. [**] Compare upper and lower motor neuron lesions.
Model answer:
- Upper motor neuron lesion: spastic (increased) tone, hyperreflexia, clonus, no significant wasting (disuse only), absent abdominal reflexes, extensor plantar (Babinski +), no fasciculations.
- Lower motor neuron lesion: flaccid (decreased) tone, hyporeflexia/areflexia, marked muscle wasting, fasciculations, flexor or absent plantar response.
- UMN weakness is in a pyramidal distribution; LMN weakness follows the affected nerve/root and segment.

ES24. [*] Describe the muscle spindle and the stretch (myotatic) reflex.
Model answer:
- The muscle spindle is an encapsulated stretch receptor of intrafusal fibers, supplied by sensory Ia/II afferents and motor gamma efferents.
- Stretching the muscle stretches the spindle, exciting Ia afferents that synapse directly (monosynaptically) on alpha motor neurons of the same muscle.
- This causes reflex contraction (e.g., the knee jerk), while inhibitory interneurons relax the antagonist (reciprocal inhibition).
- Gamma motor neurons keep the spindle sensitive across different muscle lengths.
